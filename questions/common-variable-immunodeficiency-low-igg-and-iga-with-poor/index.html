USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Common variable immunodeficiency: low IgG and IgA with poor vaccine response, immunoglobulin replacement

USMLE Step 2 CKImmunologyInternal Medicine

A 29-year-old woman comes to the physician because of her fourth episode of sinusitis in 12 months. She was hospitalized twice in the past 3 years for lobar pneumonia and had Giardia lamblia enteritis last year. She was healthy as a child. She takes no medications. Examination shows purulent nasal discharge and a palpable spleen tip. Leukocyte count and differential are normal. HIV antibody and antigen testing is negative. Serum studies show IgG 210 mg/dL (N = 650–1500), IgA 12 mg/dL (N = 75–390) and IgM 30 mg/dL (N = 40–345). Flow cytometry shows a normal number of CD19+ B cells. Four weeks after immunization with the 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine and tetanus toxoid, specific antibody titers are unchanged and below protective levels.

In addition to treating the current infection, which of the following is the most appropriate long-term management?

  1. Regular immunoglobulin replacement therapy
  2. Daily trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis alone
  3. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation
  4. Repeat immunization with pneumococcal conjugate vaccine and reassessment in 1 year
  5. Recombinant interferon gamma three times weekly
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Regular immunoglobulin replacement therapy

Recurrent sinopulmonary infections with encapsulated bacteria and Giardia enteritis beginning in adulthood, markedly low IgG with low IgA (and often IgM), normal B-cell numbers and absent responses to both protein and polysaccharide vaccines establish common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) once secondary causes (drugs, protein loss, lymphoid malignancy, HIV) are excluded. B cells are present but fail to differentiate into plasma cells. The mainstay of treatment is lifelong immunoglobulin replacement, intravenous every 3 to 4 weeks or subcutaneous weekly, which reduces the frequency of bacterial infections and slows development of bronchiectasis. Patients also need surveillance for complications: autoimmune cytopenias, granulomatous-lymphocytic interstitial lung disease, enteropathy, and a markedly increased risk of lymphoma and gastric cancer. Live vaccines are generally avoided.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Daily trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis alone — Antibiotic prophylaxis can be added for breakthrough infections or bronchiectasis but does not replace missing antibody.
  • C. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation — Stem cell transplantation is reserved for severe combined immunodeficiency and selected severe complications, not first-line therapy for CVID.
  • D. Repeat immunization with pneumococcal conjugate vaccine and reassessment in 1 year — She has already shown that she cannot mount vaccine responses; repeating immunization would delay effective therapy.
  • E. Recombinant interferon gamma three times weekly — Interferon gamma is used in chronic granulomatous disease, a phagocyte defect with catalase-positive infections and normal immunoglobulins.

Key point

Diagnose common variable immunodeficiency and select immunoglobulin replacement as long-term therapy

Memory hook

Adult, recurrent sinus and lung infections, low IgG + IgA, B cells present, vaccines don't take: CVID → replace immunoglobulin.
