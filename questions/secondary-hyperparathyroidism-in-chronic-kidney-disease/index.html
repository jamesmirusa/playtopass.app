USMLE Step 1 practice question: Secondary hyperparathyroidism in chronic kidney disease: phosphate retention and low calcitriol

USMLE Step 1EndocrinePhysiology

A 58-year-old man with chronic kidney disease due to diabetic nephropathy comes to the physician because of diffuse bone aches and itching. He is not yet receiving dialysis. His estimated glomerular filtration rate is 14 mL/min/1.73 m². Laboratory studies show: Calcium: 8.1 mg/dL Phosphorus: 6.8 mg/dL Intact parathyroid hormone: 540 pg/mL (reference range 10–65) 25-Hydroxyvitamin D: 32 ng/mL 1,25-Dihydroxyvitamin D: low Alkaline phosphatase: 210 U/L Magnesium: 2.3 mg/dL Radiographs of the hands show subperiosteal bone resorption along the radial aspects of the middle phalanges.

Which of the following mechanisms contributes most to this patient's elevated parathyroid hormone concentration?

  1. Autonomous secretion of hormone by a parathyroid adenoma
  2. Production of parathyroid hormone–related peptide by a tumor
  3. An inactivating mutation of the calcium-sensing receptor
  4. Magnesium deficiency impairing hormone secretion
  5. Phosphate retention and reduced renal 1α-hydroxylase activity
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: E. Phosphate retention and reduced renal 1α-hydroxylase activity

Low calcium, high phosphorus, a very high parathyroid hormone concentration and low 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D in advanced chronic kidney disease indicate secondary hyperparathyroidism. As nephron mass falls, phosphate excretion declines. Phosphate retention lowers ionized calcium by binding it, directly stimulates parathyroid hormone synthesis and raises FGF23 from osteocytes. FGF23 and the loss of functioning proximal tubules suppress renal 1α-hydroxylase, so calcitriol falls; this reduces intestinal calcium absorption and removes calcitriol's direct suppression of the parathyroid hormone gene. The parathyroid glands become hyperplastic. The excess hormone produces renal osteodystrophy, with subperiosteal resorption, bone pain and fractures (osteitis fibrosa cystica), and contributes to vascular calcification and pruritus. Treatment includes dietary phosphate restriction, phosphate binders, calcitriol or its analogues and calcimimetics such as cinacalcet; parathyroidectomy is reserved for refractory disease. After years of stimulation, glands can become autonomous and cause hypercalcemia (tertiary hyperparathyroidism).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Autonomous secretion of hormone by a parathyroid adenoma — An adenoma causes primary hyperparathyroidism with high calcium and low phosphorus, the opposite of this pattern.
  • B. Production of parathyroid hormone–related peptide by a tumor — PTHrP-mediated hypercalcemia of malignancy suppresses native parathyroid hormone and raises calcium.
  • C. An inactivating mutation of the calcium-sensing receptor — Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia causes high calcium with mildly raised parathyroid hormone and low urinary calcium.
  • D. Magnesium deficiency impairing hormone secretion — Severe magnesium deficiency impairs parathyroid hormone release and action, lowering the hormone; her magnesium is normal.

Key point

Explain secondary hyperparathyroidism in chronic kidney disease by phosphate retention and calcitriol deficiency

Memory hook

Failing kidneys keep phosphate and stop making calcitriol: calcium falls and the parathyroids grow.
