USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Acute nonspecific low back pain without red flags: no imaging, stay active and nonpharmacologic therapy or an NSAID

USMLE Step 2 CKMusculoskeletalInternal Medicine

A 38-year-old warehouse worker comes to the physician because of low back pain for 5 days that began after he lifted a heavy box. The pain is in the lower lumbar area, worse with bending and better with rest, and does not radiate below the knees. He has no fever, weight loss, history of cancer, injection drug use, urinary retention, fecal incontinence or leg weakness. His temperature is 36.8°C (98.2°F). Examination shows paraspinal muscle tenderness and limited forward flexion. Straight-leg raising is negative bilaterally. Strength, sensation and deep tendon reflexes in the lower extremities are normal, and perianal sensation is intact.

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. MRI of the lumbar spine
  2. Stay active; heat or an NSAID as needed
  3. Strict bed rest for 1 week
  4. Oral oxycodone for 2 weeks
  5. Radiography of the lumbar spine
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Stay active; heat or an NSAID as needed

Acute low back pain after lifting, with paraspinal tenderness, a normal neurologic examination and no red flags, is nonspecific (mechanical) low back pain, which improves substantially within a few weeks in most patients. Imaging is not recommended in the absence of red flags such as cancer history, unexplained weight loss, fever or immunosuppression, injection drug use, significant trauma or osteoporosis, progressive neurologic deficits, or signs of cauda equina syndrome; imaging does not improve outcomes, frequently shows incidental degenerative findings and leads to unnecessary procedures. The 2017 American College of Physicians guideline recommends nonpharmacologic treatment first, such as superficial heat, massage, acupuncture or spinal manipulation, and, if medication is desired, an NSAID or a skeletal muscle relaxant. Patients should be reassured and advised to remain as active as tolerated, because bed rest delays recovery. Opioids are avoided for acute low back pain except in carefully selected cases. Reevaluation is appropriate if pain persists beyond 4 to 6 weeks or red flags appear.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. MRI of the lumbar spine — MRI is reserved for red flags, progressive neurologic deficits or persistent symptoms in a candidate for surgery or injection.
  • C. Strict bed rest for 1 week — Bed rest prolongs pain and disability; patients recover faster when they stay active.
  • D. Oral oxycodone for 2 weeks — Opioids offer little benefit over NSAIDs for acute low back pain and carry risks of dependence and adverse effects.
  • E. Radiography of the lumbar spine — Plain radiographs add no useful information without red flags and expose the patient to radiation.

Key point

Manage acute nonspecific low back pain without imaging, using activity and first-line therapies

Memory hook

Back pain after lifting, no red flags: skip the scanner, keep moving, use heat or an NSAID.
