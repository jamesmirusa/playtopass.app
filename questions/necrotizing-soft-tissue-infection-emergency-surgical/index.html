USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Necrotizing soft tissue infection: emergency surgical debridement with broad-spectrum antibiotics

USMLE Step 2 CKDermatologySurgery

A 54-year-old man with type 2 diabetes is brought to the emergency department because of rapidly worsening pain and swelling of his right lower leg. Two days ago, he scraped his shin on a fence. He describes the pain as excruciating. Temperature is 39.2°C (102.6°F), pulse is 126/min, blood pressure is 90/54 mm Hg, and respirations are 24/min. The leg has tense edema and dusky erythema extending from the ankle to the knee, with scattered hemorrhagic bullae and an area of skin anesthesia; tenderness extends beyond the visible erythema. Laboratory studies show a leukocyte count of 24,600/mm³, sodium of 129 mEq/L, glucose of 342 mg/dL, creatinine of 1.9 mg/dL, and lactate of 4.1 mmol/L. IV fluids have been started, and blood cultures have been drawn.

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. MRI of the leg, then antibiotics guided by the findings
  2. IV vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam with review in 24 hours
  3. Bedside incision and drainage with oral clindamycin
  4. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy with IV penicillin
  5. Emergency surgical debridement plus broad-spectrum IV antibiotics
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: E. Emergency surgical debridement plus broad-spectrum IV antibiotics

Pain out of proportion to visible findings, rapid spread, tense edema, hemorrhagic bullae, skin anesthesia, and systemic toxicity (septic shock, hyponatremia, acute kidney injury, high lactate) indicate a necrotizing soft tissue infection (necrotizing fasciitis). After minor trauma in a patient with diabetes, it is often polymicrobial (type I), whereas group A Streptococcus causes monomicrobial type II infection. Infection spreads along the fascia faster than the overlying skin changes, and thrombosis of perforating vessels causes skin necrosis and anesthesia.

Necrotizing fasciitis is a clinical diagnosis and a surgical emergency: immediate operative exploration with debridement of all necrotic tissue is the most important determinant of survival, usually with repeat debridement every 24 to 48 hours until no necrotic tissue remains. Imaging should not delay surgery when the clinical picture is clear. Empiric IV antibiotics must cover MRSA, streptococci, gram-negative rods, and anaerobes, typically vancomycin (or linezolid) plus piperacillin-tazobactam (or a carbapenem), with clindamycin added to suppress toxin production. Scores such as LRINEC can support suspicion but cannot rule the diagnosis out.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. MRI of the leg, then antibiotics guided by the findings — Imaging delays definitive treatment; when the clinical picture is clear, surgery should not wait for MRI or CT.
  • B. IV vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam with review in 24 hours — Antibiotics alone cannot penetrate necrotic, thrombosed tissue; without debridement, mortality is extremely high.
  • C. Bedside incision and drainage with oral clindamycin — Bedside drainage is for abscesses; necrotizing infection requires operative debridement, and oral therapy is inadequate in septic shock.
  • D. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy with IV penicillin — Hyperbaric oxygen is an unproven adjunct that must never delay surgery, and penicillin alone does not cover MRSA or gram-negative organisms.

Key point

Suspected necrotizing fasciitis requires emergency surgical debridement together with broad-spectrum IV antibiotics; imaging must not delay surgery.

Memory hook

Pain beyond the redness plus shock: the scalpel is the main antibiotic. Debride now; vancomycin, piperacillin-tazobactam, and clindamycin ride along.
