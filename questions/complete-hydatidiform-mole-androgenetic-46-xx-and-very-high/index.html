USMLE Step 1 practice question: Complete hydatidiform mole: androgenetic 46,XX and very high hCG

USMLE Step 1ReproductivePathology

A 24-year-old woman, gravida 1, para 0, at 11 weeks' gestation by last menstrual period comes to the emergency department because of vaginal bleeding and severe nausea and vomiting for 1 week. Pulse is 112/min, and blood pressure is 152/96 mm Hg. Fundal height corresponds to a 16-week gestation. Laboratory studies show: β-hCG 412,000 mIU/mL TSH 0.05 μU/mL Free thyroxine 2.6 ng/dL Urine protein 2+ Transvaginal ultrasonography shows a uterus filled with a heterogeneous mass containing numerous small cystic spaces; no fetus or amniotic sac is seen. Both ovaries contain large, multiloculated cysts. Suction curettage is performed. Microscopy shows diffusely edematous, enlarged villi with central cisterns and circumferential trophoblastic proliferation. Immunostaining for p57 is negative in the villous cytotrophoblast and stroma.

Which of the following best describes the genetic composition of this tissue?

  1. 46,XX, with all chromosomes of paternal origin
  2. 69,XXY, with two paternal sets and one maternal set
  3. 46,XX, with one maternal and one paternal set
  4. 46,XX, with all chromosomes of maternal origin
  5. 47,XX,+16, with chromosomes from both parents
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. 46,XX, with all chromosomes of paternal origin

Vaginal bleeding, a uterus larger than expected for dates, hyperemesis, hypertension with proteinuria before 20 weeks' gestation, hyperthyroidism, a very high β-hCG, a snowstorm-like intrauterine mass without a fetus and bilateral theca lutein cysts indicate a complete hydatidiform mole. The histology confirms it: diffusely hydropic villi with cisterns, circumferential trophoblastic hyperplasia and absent p57 staining.

A complete mole usually arises when an ovum that has lost its maternal nuclear genome is fertilized by a single sperm whose genome then duplicates (most cases, about 80%, giving 46,XX); less often, two sperm fertilize the empty ovum (46,XX or 46,XY). All nuclear chromosomes are therefore paternal (androgenetic), although mitochondrial DNA is maternal. Because paternally expressed genes drive trophoblast growth and maternally expressed genes restrain it, the absence of a maternal genome leads to exuberant trophoblastic proliferation with no embryo. p57 (CDKN1C) is paternally imprinted and expressed only from the maternal allele, so its absence in villous cells confirms the lack of maternal DNA. The excess trophoblast produces large amounts of hCG, often more than 100,000 mIU/mL; hCG shares an alpha subunit with TSH, LH and FSH and can stimulate the TSH receptor (hyperthyroidism) and the ovaries (theca lutein cysts).

About 15% to 20% of complete moles progress to gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (invasive mole or choriocarcinoma), compared with fewer than 5% of partial moles. After evacuation, serial hCG measurements are required until levels are undetectable, with reliable contraception during follow-up. A partial mole is triploid (69,XXX or 69,XXY, with two paternal sets and one maternal set), may contain fetal parts, shows focal villous swelling and is p57 positive.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. 69,XXY, with two paternal sets and one maternal set — A triploid karyotype with two paternal sets and one maternal set describes a partial mole, which may contain fetal tissue and stains positive for p57.
  • C. 46,XX, with one maternal and one paternal set — A normal biparental karyotype would express maternal p57 and would not produce diffuse trophoblastic proliferation with this degree of hCG elevation.
  • D. 46,XX, with all chromosomes of maternal origin — An entirely maternal 46,XX genome characterizes a mature cystic teratoma of the ovary, which arises from a germ cell after the first meiotic division.
  • E. 47,XX,+16, with chromosomes from both parents — Trisomy 16 is the most common trisomy in first-trimester miscarriages; it does not cause a hydropic mass with marked hCG elevation.

Key point

A complete hydatidiform mole is androgenetic (usually 46,XX with all nuclear chromosomes paternal), lacks maternally expressed p57 and produces very high hCG levels.

Memory hook

Complete mole: all dad, no mom, no fetus. Dad's genes grow placenta, so hCG soars and p57, mom's gene, is missing.
