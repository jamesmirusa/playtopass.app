USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Major burns: modified Parkland formula fluid resuscitation timed from the burn

USMLE Step 2 CKMultisystemSurgery

A 40-year-old man is brought to the emergency department 2 hours after sustaining burns in a house fire. He weighs 80 kg. He has no hoarseness, stridor or carbonaceous sputum, and his airway is patent. Pulse is 118/min, respirations are 22/min, blood pressure is 112/70 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation is 98% on room air. Examination shows blistered, moist, painful partial-thickness burns over the entire anterior trunk and the entire right upper extremity, and dry, leathery, insensate full-thickness burns over the anterior surfaces of both lower extremities. There is painful erythema without blisters over the face. Paramedics infused 600 mL of lactated Ringer solution during transport. A urinary catheter is placed. The burn team will give lactated Ringer solution using a modified Parkland formula of 2 mL/kg per percent of total body surface area (TBSA) burned over the first 24 hours and will titrate the rate to urine output.

Which of the following is the most appropriate initial infusion rate of lactated Ringer solution?

  1. 225 mL/h
  2. 300 mL/h
  3. 375 mL/h
  4. 450 mL/h
  5. 500 mL/h
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: E. 500 mL/h

Formal fluid resuscitation is needed for burns involving about 20% or more of TBSA in adults, because burn injury triggers a systemic inflammatory response with diffuse capillary leak that produces hypovolemic (burn) shock over the first 24 to 48 hours. Only partial-thickness (second-degree) and full-thickness (third-degree) burns are counted; superficial (first-degree) burns, such as this patient's facial erythema, are excluded. By the rule of nines, the anterior trunk is 18%, an entire upper extremity is 9%, and the anterior surface of each lower extremity is 9% (18% for both), for a total of 45% TBSA.

The classic Parkland formula is 4 mL/kg per %TBSA; current ATLS and American Burn Association guidance starts adults with thermal burns at 2 mL/kg per %TBSA to limit over-resuscitation (fluid creep) and its complications, such as abdominal and extremity compartment syndromes, pulmonary edema and deepening of burn wounds. Here, 2 × 80 kg × 45 = 7,200 mL over 24 hours. Half (3,600 mL) is given in the first 8 hours, timed from the moment of the burn rather than from arrival, and the other half over the next 16 hours. Two hours have already passed and 600 mL has been given, so 3,000 mL remains for the next 6 hours: 500 mL/h.

The formula only sets the starting rate. The infusion is then adjusted hourly to a urine output of 0.5 mL/kg/h in adults (about 30 to 50 mL/h) and about 1 mL/kg/h in children weighing less than 30 kg. Children start at 3 mL/kg per %TBSA with added dextrose-containing maintenance fluid, and electrical injuries start at 4 mL/kg per %TBSA with a urine output goal of about 1 mL/kg/h until any myoglobinuria clears. Albumin may be considered in large burns or when crystalloid requirements escalate.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 225 mL/h — 225 mL/h is the rate for hours 8 to 24 (3,600 mL over 16 hours); the first half must be given faster.
  • B. 300 mL/h — 300 mL/h spreads the 24-hour volume evenly and under-resuscitates during the first 8 hours, when capillary leak is greatest.
  • C. 375 mL/h — 375 mL/h subtracts the prehospital fluid but times the first 8 hours from arrival instead of from the burn.
  • D. 450 mL/h — 450 mL/h ignores both the 2 hours already elapsed since the burn and the 600 mL given by paramedics.

Key point

In adult thermal burns, calculate lactated Ringer volume from partial- and full-thickness TBSA (starting at 2 mL/kg per %TBSA), give half within 8 hours of the burn, and titrate to a urine output of 0.5 mL/kg/h.

Memory hook

Count blisters and leather, not sunburn. Half the fluid in the first 8 hours from the burn, not from the door.
