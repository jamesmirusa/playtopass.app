USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: New HIV diagnosis with refusal to inform a partner: public health partner notification

USMLE Step 2 CKEthicsEthics

A 32-year-old man comes to the physician to discuss the results of testing done for a sexually transmitted infection screen. HIV antibody/antigen and confirmatory tests are positive, and his viral load is 84,000 copies/mL. He has been in a sexual relationship with a woman for the past year and they do not use condoms. After extensive counseling about transmission and the benefits of disclosure, he refuses to tell her about his diagnosis because he fears she will leave him, and he asks the physician to keep the result secret from everyone.

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

  1. Keep the diagnosis confidential as the patient requests
  2. Report the case to the health department for partner notification
  3. Telephone the partner directly without telling the patient
  4. Discharge the patient from the practice for refusing to protect his partner
  5. Ask the partner's own physician to tell her at her next visit
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Report the case to the health department for partner notification

HIV infection is reportable to public health authorities in every US state, so the physician cannot promise to keep the diagnosis from everyone. Confidentiality is a strong duty, but it has limits when an identifiable third party faces a serious, preventable risk; an unprotected sexual partner of a person with untreated HIV is such a case. The physician should continue to encourage the patient to disclose, start antiretroviral therapy promptly (sustained viral suppression prevents sexual transmission), and tell him that the case will be reported. Health department partner services then notify exposed partners confidentially, without revealing who named them, and offer testing, counseling and preexposure prophylaxis. Some states also allow physicians to notify partners directly, but working through public health partner services is the standard approach and protects both the partner and the therapeutic relationship. Ending care would harm the patient and does nothing to protect the partner.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Keep the diagnosis confidential as the patient requests — The diagnosis is legally reportable, and keeping it from everyone would leave the partner at continued risk of infection.
  • C. Telephone the partner directly without telling the patient — Calling the partner behind the patient's back damages trust and bypasses the established, anonymous notification process.
  • D. Discharge the patient from the practice for refusing to protect his partner — Ending care abandons the patient, delays treatment that would reduce transmission and does not protect the partner.
  • E. Ask the partner's own physician to tell her at her next visit — Passing the diagnosis to another physician is an unauthorized disclosure and does not follow the public health process.

Key point

Use public health partner notification when a patient with HIV refuses to inform an at-risk partner

Memory hook

He will not tell her, you cannot keep it from everyone: report it, and public health tells her without naming him.
