USMLE Step 1 practice question: Hereditary angioedema: C1 inhibitor deficiency and bradykinin

USMLE Step 1ImmunologyImmunology

A 19-year-old man comes to the emergency department because of swelling of his upper lip and left hand that began 10 hours ago after a dental cleaning. He has had similar episodes since age 12, each lasting 2 to 4 days, and several episodes of severe crampy abdominal pain with vomiting. The swelling does not itch, and he has never had hives. Antihistamines and an intramuscular epinephrine injection given during a previous episode did not help. His father has had similar episodes. Examination shows nonpitting, nonerythematous swelling of the upper lip and dorsum of the left hand; there is no urticaria or stridor. Serum C4 concentration is decreased, and C1 inhibitor antigen level is low.

Which of the following mediators is primarily responsible for this patient's swelling?

  1. Histamine released from mast cells
  2. Leukotriene C4
  3. Bradykinin
  4. Complement fragment C5a
  5. Tumor necrosis factor-α
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Bradykinin

Recurrent nonpruritic, nonpitting swelling without urticaria, abdominal attacks caused by bowel wall edema, a trigger such as dental work, autosomal dominant family history, low C4 and low C1 inhibitor indicate hereditary angioedema. C1 inhibitor is a serine protease inhibitor that controls not only the classical complement pathway but also the contact (kallikrein-kinin) system. Without enough C1 inhibitor, activated factor XII and plasma kallikrein act unchecked, kallikrein cleaves high-molecular-weight kininogen, and bradykinin is released. Bradykinin binds B2 receptors on endothelium and increases vascular permeability, causing deep swelling of the skin, larynx and bowel.

Because the mediator is bradykinin rather than histamine, attacks do not respond to antihistamines, glucocorticoids or epinephrine. Acute attacks are treated with C1 inhibitor concentrate, the bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist icatibant, or the kallikrein inhibitor ecallantide; laryngeal swelling can be fatal and requires airway protection. ACE inhibitors, which slow bradykinin breakdown, must be avoided. Low C4 reflects continuous consumption of C4 by uncontrolled C1 activity.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Histamine released from mast cells — Histamine-mediated angioedema is usually accompanied by urticaria and itching and responds to antihistamines and epinephrine, unlike in this patient.
  • B. Leukotriene C4 — Leukotriene C4 contributes to bronchoconstriction and vascular leak in allergic and aspirin-exacerbated reactions, not to C1 inhibitor deficiency.
  • D. Complement fragment C5a — C5a is a chemoattractant and anaphylatoxin; the swelling in hereditary angioedema is not driven by terminal complement activation.
  • E. Tumor necrosis factor-α — TNF-α mediates systemic inflammation and septic shock, not recurrent episodic angioedema with low C4.

Key point

Hereditary angioedema results from C1 inhibitor deficiency with excess bradykinin; swelling without urticaria, low C4, no response to antihistamines or epinephrine, avoid ACE inhibitors.

Memory hook

No hives, no itch, low C4: it is bradykinin, not histamine. Treat with C1 inhibitor or icatibant, never an ACE inhibitor.
