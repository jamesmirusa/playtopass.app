USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state: isotonic saline before insulin

USMLE Step 2 CKEndocrineEmergency Medicine

A 74-year-old man with type 2 diabetes mellitus is brought from a nursing home because of 5 days of increasing confusion, frequent urination and poor oral intake. He takes metformin and glipizide. His temperature is 37.3°C (99.1°F), pulse is 118/min, respirations are 18/min, and blood pressure is 92/56 mm Hg. He is lethargic but arousable, with dry mucous membranes and no focal neurologic deficits. Laboratory studies show: Glucose: 1040 mg/dL Sodium: 148 mEq/L Potassium: 4.6 mEq/L Bicarbonate: 23 mEq/L Urea nitrogen: 64 mg/dL Creatinine: 2.1 mg/dL β-Hydroxybutyrate: 0.6 mmol/L (normal <0.6) Arterial pH: 7.36 Calculated effective serum osmolality is 340 mOsm/kg.

Which of the following is the most appropriate initial step in management?

  1. Intravenous 0.9% saline, about 1 L over the first hour
  2. Intravenous regular insulin bolus followed by an infusion
  3. Intravenous 0.45% saline
  4. Intravenous 5% dextrose in water
  5. Subcutaneous insulin glargine
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Intravenous 0.9% saline, about 1 L over the first hour

Extreme hyperglycemia, markedly raised effective osmolality, altered mental status and severe dehydration without significant ketosis or acidosis define the hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state. Residual insulin secretion suppresses ketogenesis, so hyperglycemia and osmotic diuresis progress over days and the fluid deficit is often 8 to 10 L. The first priority is restoring intravascular volume with isotonic saline, roughly 15 to 20 mL/kg in the first hour, as recommended in the 2024 ADA consensus on hyperglycemic crises. Fluid alone lowers glucose substantially by dilution and improved renal excretion. After initial resuscitation, 0.45% saline is used if the corrected sodium concentration is normal or high (here it is about 163 mEq/L). Insulin is added once fluids are under way and potassium is at least 3.5 mEq/L, usually at a lower rate than in ketoacidosis, and dextrose is added when glucose falls to about 300 mg/dL. Glucose and osmolality should fall gradually to limit the risk of cerebral edema.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Intravenous regular insulin bolus followed by an infusion — Insulin given before volume repletion drives glucose and water into cells and can worsen hypotension and shock.
  • C. Intravenous 0.45% saline — Hypotonic saline is used after initial resuscitation, when corrected sodium is normal or high; it expands plasma volume poorly in a hypotensive patient.
  • D. Intravenous 5% dextrose in water — Dextrose is added later, when glucose has fallen to about 300 mg/dL; it has no role at a glucose of 1040 mg/dL.
  • E. Subcutaneous insulin glargine — Basal subcutaneous insulin acts slowly, is absorbed unreliably in a dehydrated patient and is started only during transition off intravenous insulin.

Key point

Begin treatment of the hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state with isotonic saline before insulin

Memory hook

Glucose over 1000 but no acid: he is mostly short of water, so the first bag is saline, not insulin.
