USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Acute acetaminophen overdose: Rumack-Matthew nomogram and N-acetylcysteine

USMLE Step 2 CKMultisystemEmergency Medicine

A 19-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department by her roommate 8 hours after she ingested approximately thirty 500-mg tablets of acetaminophen following an argument with her boyfriend. The time of ingestion was witnessed. She denies ingesting any other substances. She has nausea and has vomited once. She has no history of medical illness and takes no medications. She weighs 60 kg (132 lb). Temperature is 36.9°C (98.4°F), pulse is 96/min, respirations are 16/min, and blood pressure is 118/72 mm Hg. She is alert and oriented. Abdominal examination shows no abnormalities. Laboratory studies show: Serum acetaminophen (8 hours after ingestion): 110 µg/mL Serum salicylate: undetectable Serum ethanol: undetectable AST: 34 U/L ALT: 29 U/L INR: 1.0 Creatinine: 0.8 mg/dL Bicarbonate: 23 mEq/L Urine pregnancy test: negative

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. Activated charcoal and repeat acetaminophen level in 4 hours
  2. Repeat acetaminophen level in 4 hours before deciding on therapy
  3. Hemodialysis
  4. Medical clearance and transfer for psychiatric admission
  5. Intravenous acetylcysteine
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: E. Intravenous acetylcysteine

For a single acute ingestion with a known time, the serum acetaminophen concentration measured 4–24 hours after ingestion is plotted on the Rumack-Matthew nomogram. In the United States, the treatment line begins at 150 µg/mL at 4 hours and declines with a 4-hour half-life: about 75 µg/mL at 8 hours and 37.5 µg/mL at 12 hours. Her 8-hour level of 110 µg/mL lies well above the line, indicating a risk of hepatotoxicity, so N-acetylcysteine (NAC) is indicated. Her ingestion (15 g, or 250 mg/kg) also far exceeds the potentially toxic threshold of about 150 mg/kg. Normal aminotransferases at 8 hours are expected and do not exclude toxicity: liver injury becomes apparent at 24–36 hours and peaks at 72–96 hours.

In overdose, glucuronidation and sulfation become saturated, so more acetaminophen is oxidized by CYP2E1 to the reactive metabolite NAPQI. When hepatic glutathione is depleted, NAPQI binds hepatocellular proteins and causes centrilobular (zone 3) necrosis. NAC restores glutathione (as a cysteine precursor), directly detoxifies NAPQI, and enhances sulfation. It is nearly completely protective when started within 8 hours of ingestion, and efficacy declines thereafter, so it should be started immediately. Intravenous regimens include the 21-hour three-bag protocol (150 mg/kg over 1 hour, 50 mg/kg over 4 hours, then 100 mg/kg over 16 hours) and the two-bag protocol (200 mg/kg over 4 hours, then 100 mg/kg over 16 hours); a 72-hour oral regimen is an alternative. Anaphylactoid reactions (flushing, urticaria, bronchospasm) occur mostly during the loading dose and are managed by pausing the infusion and giving antihistamines. Before stopping NAC, the acetaminophen concentration and aminotransferases are rechecked; NAC is continued if acetaminophen is still detectable or aminotransferases are elevated or rising.

Activated charcoal is generally considered within about 4 hours of ingestion (sometimes later for very large or extended-release ingestions) and must never delay NAC. Hemodialysis is reserved for massive ingestions with very high concentrations accompanied by altered mental status, metabolic acidosis, or elevated lactate. A higher NAC dose is recommended when the concentration lies above a line parallel to and twice the treatment line (300-µg/mL line). After medical treatment, every patient with an intentional overdose needs a psychiatric suicide risk assessment.

High-yield summary: acute single ingestion → level at ≥4 hours on the nomogram (150 at 4 h, 75 at 8 h, 37.5 at 12 h) → above the line → NAC. Unknown time of ingestion or repeated supratherapeutic ingestion → treat if acetaminophen is detectable or AST is elevated. Level not available within 8 hours of a potentially toxic ingestion → start NAC empirically while awaiting results.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Activated charcoal and repeat acetaminophen level in 4 hours — Activated charcoal is generally beneficial only within about 4 hours of an immediate-release ingestion, and waiting 4 more hours for a repeat level would delay NAC beyond the period when it is most effective.
  • B. Repeat acetaminophen level in 4 hours before deciding on therapy — A single level drawn ≥4 hours after a known ingestion time is sufficient for decision-making; this level is already above the treatment line, and delaying NAC increases the risk of hepatotoxicity.
  • C. Hemodialysis — Hemodialysis is reserved for massive ingestions with very high concentrations and signs of mitochondrial dysfunction (coma, metabolic acidosis, elevated lactate); this patient is alert with normal bicarbonate.
  • D. Medical clearance and transfer for psychiatric admission — Normal aminotransferases at 8 hours do not exclude toxicity, which appears after 24–36 hours. She needs NAC first; psychiatric evaluation follows medical treatment.

Key point

An acute acetaminophen ingestion with a serum level above the Rumack-Matthew treatment line (150 µg/mL at 4 hours, about 75 µg/mL at 8 hours) requires prompt N-acetylcysteine, regardless of normal initial aminotransferases.

Memory hook

150 at 4, then halve every 4 hours: 75 at 8, 37.5 at 12. Above the line? Start NAC — normal early LFTs mean nothing.
