USMLE Step 1 practice question: Chronic aortic regurgitation

USMLE Step 1CardiovascularPhysiology

A 57-year-old man comes to the physician because of a 6-month history of progressive shortness of breath on exertion and an uncomfortable awareness of a forceful heartbeat, especially when he lies on his left side. He was told he had a heart murmur during a sports physical examination in college but never had follow-up. He does not smoke and drinks alcohol occasionally. His pulse is 82/min and regular, and blood pressure is 164/48 mm Hg. The carotid pulses are bounding, with a rapid upstroke followed by rapid collapse, and pulsations of the nail beds are visible with gentle pressure on the fingertips. The point of maximal impulse is diffuse and displaced to the left anterior axillary line in the sixth intercostal space. Auscultation shows a high-pitched, blowing, decrescendo diastolic murmur that begins immediately after S2 and is heard best along the left sternal border with the patient sitting up, leaning forward, and holding his breath in expiration. The lungs are clear to auscultation.

Which of the following best describes the hemodynamic changes in this patient's left ventricle?

  1. Decreased end-diastolic volume with decreased stroke volume
  2. Normal end-diastolic volume with concentric wall thickening
  3. Systolic ejection of blood into the left atrium with large v waves
  4. Increased end-diastolic volume with increased total stroke volume
  5. Prolonged isovolumetric relaxation with elevated aortic diastolic pressure
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Increased end-diastolic volume with increased total stroke volume

This patient has chronic aortic regurgitation (AR), suggested by a wide pulse pressure (164/48 mm Hg), bounding “water-hammer” (Corrigan) pulses, capillary pulsations of the nail beds (Quincke sign), a diffuse, laterally displaced apical impulse, and a high-pitched, blowing, early diastolic decrescendo murmur heard best along the left sternal border with the patient leaning forward at end-expiration. A murmur detected in young adulthood suggests a congenital bicuspid aortic valve, a common cause of AR in developed countries.

In AR, part of each stroke volume flows back from the aorta into the left ventricle (LV) during diastole. The LV therefore fills from two sources (the left atrium and the regurgitant jet), which produces chronic volume overload. The LV adapts by adding sarcomeres in series (eccentric hypertrophy), which dilates the chamber and markedly increases end-diastolic volume (EDV). Through the Frank-Starling mechanism, the enlarged EDV allows the LV to eject a very large total stroke volume (forward plus regurgitant volume), which raises systolic pressure. Rapid diastolic runoff of blood back into the LV (and into the periphery) lowers aortic diastolic pressure, producing the wide pulse pressure and collapsing pulse. Because the aortic valve never seals completely, there is no true isovolumetric relaxation phase on the pressure-volume loop.

In the compensated stage, the ejection fraction is preserved and patients can remain asymptomatic for years. Persistent volume and pressure overload (Laplace law: wall stress rises with chamber radius) eventually causes LV systolic dysfunction; symptoms, an LV ejection fraction ≤55%, or marked LV dilation are indications for aortic valve replacement.

High-yield summary: Chronic AR = volume overload → eccentric hypertrophy, ↑EDV, ↑total stroke volume, wide pulse pressure, and loss of isovolumetric relaxation. Aortic stenosis = pressure overload → concentric hypertrophy with a normal or small EDV.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Decreased end-diastolic volume with decreased stroke volume — Decreased EDV and stroke volume occur with impaired LV filling (e.g., hemorrhage, mitral stenosis, constrictive pericarditis), not with the regurgitant volume load of AR, which increases both.
  • B. Normal end-diastolic volume with concentric wall thickening — Concentric hypertrophy with a normal-sized cavity is the response to pressure overload, as in aortic stenosis or long-standing hypertension; aortic stenosis causes a crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur and a weak, delayed pulse (pulsus parvus et tardus).
  • C. Systolic ejection of blood into the left atrium with large v waves — Systolic regurgitation into the left atrium with prominent v waves is characteristic of mitral regurgitation, which causes a holosystolic apical murmur radiating to the axilla rather than an early diastolic murmur.
  • E. Prolonged isovolumetric relaxation with elevated aortic diastolic pressure — In AR, aortic diastolic pressure is low because of rapid runoff into the LV, and the isovolumetric relaxation phase is lost because the incompetent valve allows LV filling to begin immediately after ejection.

Key point

Chronic aortic regurgitation causes LV volume overload with eccentric hypertrophy, increased end-diastolic volume, and increased total stroke volume, producing a wide pulse pressure and an early diastolic decrescendo murmur.

Memory hook

Regurgitation = 'double refill': the LV fills from atrium AND aorta → balloons (eccentric, ↑EDV) → huge stroke volume → water-hammer pulse. Stenosis thickens walls.
