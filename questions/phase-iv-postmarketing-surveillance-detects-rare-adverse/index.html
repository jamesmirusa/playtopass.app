USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Phase IV postmarketing surveillance detects rare adverse drug effects

USMLE Step 2 CKBiostatisticsEpidemiology

A new oral anticoagulant was approved after a phase III randomized trial of 9200 patients showed noninferiority to warfarin with fewer intracranial hemorrhages. Two years after approval, during which about 850,000 patients have taken the drug, reports to the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System and analyses of insurance claims data identify an increased risk of severe drug-induced liver injury, occurring in about 1 in 12,000 treated patients. No cases occurred in the phase III trial.

The detection of this adverse effect is best explained by which of the following?

  1. Phase I studies in healthy volunteers were too short
  2. Phase II studies used the wrong dose
  3. Phase IV surveillance includes enough patients to detect rare events
  4. Phase III trials are not randomized
  5. Phase III trials measure efficacy but never record adverse events
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Phase IV surveillance includes enough patients to detect rare events

Drug development proceeds through phases. Phase I studies give the drug to a small number of usually healthy volunteers (tens) to establish safety, pharmacokinetics and dosing. Phase II studies treat a few hundred patients with the disease to assess efficacy and optimal dose and to identify common adverse effects. Phase III trials randomize hundreds to thousands of patients to compare the drug with placebo or standard treatment and form the basis for approval. Even large phase III trials are too small to detect rare adverse effects: by the rule of three, if no events occur in n patients, the upper limit of the 95% confidence interval for the event rate is about 3/n, so a trial of 9200 patients can exclude only events more common than about 1 in 3000. Phase IV (postmarketing) surveillance, through spontaneous reporting systems, registries and large health care databases, follows far more patients, including older patients and those with comorbidities who were excluded from trials, and can detect rare or delayed adverse effects, which may lead to label changes or withdrawal.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Phase I studies in healthy volunteers were too short — Phase I studies involve too few people to detect an event occurring in 1 in 12,000 patients, regardless of duration.
  • B. Phase II studies used the wrong dose — Dose-finding in phase II involves a few hundred patients and could not detect such a rare event.
  • D. Phase III trials are not randomized — Phase III trials are typically randomized; the limitation here is sample size, not design.
  • E. Phase III trials measure efficacy but never record adverse events — Phase III trials do record adverse events; they simply cannot detect very rare ones.

Key point

Explain why rare adverse effects are detected in phase IV postmarketing surveillance rather than in phase III trials

Memory hook

Zero cases in 9200 trial patients, then liver injury in 1 of 12,000: only phase IV has enough patients to see the rare harm.
