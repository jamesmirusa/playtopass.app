USMLE Step 1 practice question: Hereditary hemochromatosis: HFE C282Y and low hepcidin

USMLE Step 1GastrointestinalGenetics

A 52-year-old man comes to the physician because of fatigue, decreased libido and joint pain for 1 year. He rarely drinks alcohol. His skin is diffusely tanned although he avoids the sun. The second and third metacarpophalangeal joints of both hands are tender and enlarged, and the liver edge is palpable 3 cm below the costal margin. Laboratory studies show: Hemoglobin 15.2 g/dL Fasting glucose 186 mg/dL ALT 76 U/L AST 61 U/L Transferrin saturation 72% Ferritin 1,480 ng/mL

Which of the following best explains this patient's iron overload?

  1. Inappropriately low hepatic hepcidin production
  2. Cytokine-induced increase in hepcidin production
  3. Ineffective erythropoiesis requiring transfusions
  4. Defective biliary excretion of copper by ATP7B
  5. Defective iron incorporation into protoporphyrin
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Inappropriately low hepatic hepcidin production

Fatigue, hypogonadism, bronze skin, arthropathy of the second and third metacarpophalangeal joints, new diabetes and hepatomegaly with raised aminotransferases, together with a high transferrin saturation and ferritin and a normal hemoglobin, indicate hereditary hemochromatosis. Men usually present in midlife; women present later because menstruation and pregnancy remove iron.

The disease is autosomal recessive and is most often caused by homozygosity for the C282Y variant of HFE on chromosome 6, close to the HLA-A locus. C282Y disrupts a disulfide bond, so HFE cannot bind beta-2 microglobulin or reach the cell surface. HFE normally works with transferrin receptor 2 and hemojuvelin to sense body iron and signal (through the BMP-SMAD pathway) for hepatocytes to make hepcidin. With too little hepcidin, the iron exporter ferroportin stays on duodenal enterocytes and macrophages, and dietary iron is absorbed and released without restraint. Iron accumulates in parenchymal cells of the liver, pancreas, heart, pituitary, skin and joints, where it generates free radicals through the Fenton reaction. Penetrance is incomplete, and alcohol accelerates liver injury.

Complications include cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias. Treatment is regular phlebotomy, with chelation (deferoxamine, deferasirox) reserved for patients who cannot undergo it, and first-degree relatives should be screened. Rarer forms of hereditary iron overload involve hemojuvelin, hepcidin itself or transferrin receptor 2.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Cytokine-induced increase in hepcidin production — High hepcidin driven by IL-6 causes anemia of chronic disease, with low serum iron and low transferrin saturation, the opposite of this pattern.
  • C. Ineffective erythropoiesis requiring transfusions — Transfusional iron overload occurs in thalassemia major or myelodysplasia; this patient has no anemia and no transfusion history.
  • D. Defective biliary excretion of copper by ATP7B — Wilson disease is a copper disorder of younger patients with low ceruloplasmin, neuropsychiatric signs and Kayser-Fleischer rings, not high transferrin saturation.
  • E. Defective iron incorporation into protoporphyrin — Sideroblastic anemia can cause iron overload, but it presents with microcytic anemia and ringed sideroblasts; this patient's hemoglobin is normal.

Key point

Hereditary hemochromatosis (usually autosomal recessive HFE C282Y homozygosity) causes inappropriately low hepcidin, so ferroportin-mediated iron absorption is unregulated and iron deposits in the liver, pancreas, skin, joints, heart and pituitary.

Memory hook

C282Y silences hepcidin, so ferroportin's gate stays open: bronze skin, diabetes, cirrhosis and knuckle arthritis. Bleed the patient to fix it.
