USMLE Step 1 practice question: Midgut malrotation with volvulus: incomplete 270° rotation around the superior mesenteric artery

USMLE Step 1GastrointestinalAnatomy

A 4-day-old boy is brought to the emergency department because of 3 episodes of bright green vomiting during the past 6 hours. He was born at 39 weeks' gestation after an uncomplicated pregnancy and vaginal delivery. He passed meconium within the first 24 hours of life and had been breastfeeding well until this morning. He appears irritable. His temperature is 37.3°C (99.1°F), pulse is 184/min, respirations are 54/min, and blood pressure is 64/38 mm Hg. The abdomen is mildly distended and diffusely tender. Stool obtained on rectal examination is positive for occult blood. An abdominal x-ray shows a few gas-filled loops of proximal bowel with a paucity of gas distally. An upper gastrointestinal contrast study shows the duodenojejunal junction to the right of the vertebral column, with the proximal jejunum forming a spiral configuration.

This patient's condition most likely results from an abnormality in which of the following developmental processes?

  1. Recanalization of the duodenal lumen during the second month of development
  2. Obliteration of the vitelline duct connecting the midgut to the yolk sac
  3. Migration of neural crest cells into the wall of the distal colon
  4. Return of the physiologically herniated midgut into the abdominal cavity
  5. Rotation of the midgut loop around the axis of the superior mesenteric artery
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: E. Rotation of the midgut loop around the axis of the superior mesenteric artery

Bilious vomiting in a neonate who previously fed well, together with tachycardia, abdominal tenderness, and occult blood in the stool, indicates midgut volvulus until proven otherwise. The upper GI series confirms malrotation: the duodenojejunal junction (ligament of Treitz), which normally lies to the left of the vertebral body at the level of the duodenal bulb, is displaced to the right, and the spiral (corkscrew) appearance of the proximal jejunum indicates twisting of the bowel around its mesentery.

During week 6 of development, rapid growth of the midgut forces it to herniate into the umbilical cord (physiologic herniation). While herniated, the midgut loop rotates 90° counterclockwise (viewed from the front) around the axis formed by the superior mesenteric artery (SMA). During weeks 10–12, the intestines return to the abdominal cavity and rotate an additional 180°, completing a total of 270° of counterclockwise rotation. This places the duodenojejunal junction in the left upper quadrant and the cecum in the right lower quadrant, giving the small bowel mesentery a broad base that runs obliquely from the left upper to the right lower quadrant. In malrotation, rotation is incomplete: the cecum remains high in the right upper quadrant, attached to the right abdominal wall by peritoneal (Ladd) bands that cross over and can compress the duodenum, and the mesentery has a narrow base that allows the midgut to twist around the SMA (volvulus). Volvulus obstructs both the bowel and the SMA and superior mesenteric vein, putting the entire midgut territory (distal duodenum to the proximal two-thirds of the transverse colon) at risk of ischemic necrosis.

Midgut volvulus is a surgical emergency; treatment is emergency laparotomy with detorsion, division of Ladd bands, widening of the mesenteric base, and appendectomy (Ladd procedure).

High-yield summary: Normal midgut rotation = 270° counterclockwise around the SMA. Malrotation → high cecum, Ladd bands, and a narrow mesenteric base → volvulus with bilious vomiting and midgut ischemia. Duodenal atresia = failed recanalization (double bubble, Down syndrome); jejunal atresia = vascular accident (apple peel); Meckel diverticulum = persistent vitelline duct; omphalocele = failed return of the herniated midgut.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Recanalization of the duodenal lumen during the second month of development — Failed recanalization of the duodenum causes duodenal atresia, which presents within hours of birth with bilious vomiting (if distal to the ampulla) and a double bubble sign on x-ray; it is strongly associated with Down syndrome and does not cause a spiral jejunum or bowel ischemia.
  • B. Obliteration of the vitelline duct connecting the midgut to the yolk sac — Failure of vitelline duct obliteration causes Meckel diverticulum (or a vitelline fistula or fibrous band); Meckel diverticulum usually presents in young children with painless rectal bleeding from ectopic gastric mucosa, although a persistent band can occasionally act as an axis for volvulus.
  • C. Migration of neural crest cells into the wall of the distal colon — Failed migration of neural crest cells causes Hirschsprung disease, which presents with delayed passage of meconium (>48 hours), abdominal distension, and an empty rectum; this infant passed meconium normally.
  • D. Return of the physiologically herniated midgut into the abdominal cavity — Failure of the herniated midgut to return to the abdominal cavity causes omphalocele, a midline abdominal wall defect with bowel covered by a peritoneal-amniotic membrane that is obvious at birth.

Key point

Malrotation results from incomplete 270° counterclockwise rotation of the midgut around the superior mesenteric artery and predisposes to midgut volvulus, which presents with bilious vomiting in neonates.

Memory hook

Midgut turns 270° counterclockwise around the SMA; incomplete turn → high cecum, Ladd bands, narrow stalk, volvulus. Neonatal green vomit = emergency.
