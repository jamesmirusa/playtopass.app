USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Disclosure of a medication error to the patient

USMLE Step 2 CKEthicsEthics

A 66-year-old man is hospitalized for community-acquired pneumonia. He has type 2 diabetes treated with insulin glargine. This morning, the resident physician realizes that he mistakenly entered an order for 40 units of insulin glargine instead of the patient's usual 14 units, and the dose was given last night. At 6 AM, the patient was found diaphoretic with a capillary glucose concentration of 46 mg/dL; he was treated with intravenous dextrose and is now alert, feels well and has a glucose concentration of 128 mg/dL. He is expected to recover fully and to be discharged in 2 days. He asks the resident why his blood sugar dropped so low overnight. The attending physician has been informed.

Which of the following is the most appropriate response by the resident?

  1. Explain that low glucose levels are common during acute infections
  2. Tell him the risk management office will contact him about the event
  3. Disclose the error to his wife and let her decide whether to tell him
  4. Wait until discharge to discuss the event, after he has fully recovered
  5. Explain that the insulin dose was ordered incorrectly and apologize
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: E. Explain that the insulin dose was ordered incorrectly and apologize

Physicians have an ethical duty to disclose errors that reach the patient, even when the harm is minor or temporary (AMA Code of Medical Ethics Opinion 8.6). Disclosure should be prompt, honest and in plain language, given by the physician involved and ideally together with the attending: state that an error occurred, explain what happened and how it affected the patient's health, express regret or apologize, describe what is being done to correct the consequences and to prevent recurrence, and answer the patient's questions. Concern about legal liability must not affect honesty with the patient. Transparent disclosure preserves trust and is associated with fewer, not more, lawsuits, and many states have apology laws that make expressions of sympathy inadmissible as evidence of liability.

The error should also be reported through the institution's patient safety reporting system so that system causes (such as order-entry design) can be addressed, but reporting supplements, rather than replaces, disclosure to the patient. A competent adult is entitled to information about his own care; disclosing to a family member instead violates his autonomy and confidentiality unless he has asked for that. Delaying disclosure or offering a misleading explanation is deception, even when well intentioned.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Explain that low glucose levels are common during acute infections — Attributing the event to the infection is deceptive; the patient is entitled to an honest explanation of the error.
  • B. Tell him the risk management office will contact him about the event — Incident reporting is appropriate but does not replace the physician's own prompt, direct disclosure to the patient.
  • C. Disclose the error to his wife and let her decide whether to tell him — A competent patient has the right to information about his own care; disclosing to family instead violates his autonomy and confidentiality.
  • D. Wait until discharge to discuss the event, after he has fully recovered — Disclosure should be prompt, especially when he is asking now; delay erodes trust and is not justified by his recovery.

Key point

Medical errors that reach the patient must be disclosed promptly and honestly to the patient, with an apology and an explanation of corrective steps, even when harm is temporary.

Memory hook

Error reached the patient? Tell him, say sorry, explain the fix. Honesty first; fear of liability never decides.
