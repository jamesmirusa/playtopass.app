USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Insect sting anaphylaxis: epinephrine autoinjector and referral for venom immunotherapy

USMLE Step 2 CKImmunologyEmergency Medicine

A 38-year-old landscaper is observed in the emergency department 6 hours after he was stung on the forearm by a yellow jacket. Within 10 minutes of the sting he developed generalized hives, throat tightness, vomiting and lightheadedness; paramedics recorded a blood pressure of 78/44 mm Hg and gave intramuscular epinephrine. He now feels well. Pulse is 82/min and blood pressure is 122/76 mm Hg. The lungs are clear, and only a 3-cm area of redness remains at the sting site. He was stung 2 years ago and had only local swelling. He is given a prescription for an epinephrine autoinjector and shown how to use it.

Which of the following additional steps is most likely to reduce this patient's risk of a life-threatening reaction to a future sting?

  1. Daily oral cetirizine during the summer months
  2. Referral to an allergist for venom testing and venom immunotherapy
  3. A supply of oral prednisone to take immediately after any sting
  4. Reassurance that a second systemic reaction is unlikely
  5. Measurement of serum tryptase now and no further evaluation
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Referral to an allergist for venom testing and venom immunotherapy

Generalized urticaria with airway symptoms, vomiting and hypotension minutes after a Hymenoptera sting is anaphylaxis, an IgE-mediated (type I) reaction. An adult with a systemic sting reaction has a substantial risk, roughly 30% to 60%, of another systemic reaction when stung again. Every such patient should carry an epinephrine autoinjector and be referred to an allergist for venom-specific IgE testing (skin or serum). If sensitization is confirmed, subcutaneous venom immunotherapy lowers the risk of a future systemic reaction to under 5% and is usually continued for 3 to 5 years. Testing is best done a few weeks after the reaction, because results can be falsely negative immediately afterward. Large local reactions alone do not require immunotherapy.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Daily oral cetirizine during the summer months — Antihistamines relieve itching and hives but do not prevent or treat anaphylaxis.
  • C. A supply of oral prednisone to take immediately after any sting — Glucocorticoids act slowly and have not been shown to prevent anaphylaxis; epinephrine is the only first-line treatment.
  • D. Reassurance that a second systemic reaction is unlikely — The risk of another systemic reaction is high in adults, so reassurance alone is unsafe.
  • E. Measurement of serum tryptase now and no further evaluation — A raised baseline tryptase can point to mastocytosis and a higher risk, but measuring it does not replace allergy evaluation and immunotherapy.

Key point

Refer patients with systemic Hymenoptera sting reactions for venom testing and venom immunotherapy

Memory hook

Survived the sting thanks to epinephrine? Next stop is the allergist: venom shots cut the repeat risk to under 5%.
