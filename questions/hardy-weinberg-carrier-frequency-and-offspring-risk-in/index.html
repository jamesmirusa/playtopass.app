USMLE Step 1 practice question: Hardy-Weinberg: carrier frequency and offspring risk in phenylketonuria

USMLE Step 1GeneticsGenetics

A 28-year-old woman is found to be heterozygous for a pathogenic variant in the phenylalanine hydroxylase gene on expanded carrier screening before conception. She has no family history of phenylketonuria. Her husband is healthy, has no family history of the disease and declines testing. Both are from a large, randomly mating population in which phenylketonuria occurs in 1 of every 10,000 newborns.

Assuming the population is in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, which of the following is the probability that their first child will have phenylketonuria?

  1. 1 in 50
  2. 1 in 100
  3. 1 in 200
  4. 1 in 400
  5. 1 in 40,000
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. 1 in 200

In Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, the frequency of an autosomal recessive disease equals q², where q is the frequency of the disease allele. Here q² = 1/10,000, so q = 1/100 and p = 1 − q ≈ 1. The carrier (heterozygote) frequency is 2pq ≈ 2q = 2/100 = 1/50.

The woman is a known carrier (probability 1). Her husband, with no family history, has the population carrier risk of 1/50. If both parents are carriers, each child has a 1/4 chance of being affected. The probability for the first child is therefore 1 × 1/50 × 1/4 = 1/200.

Two shortcuts are worth remembering. For rare recessive diseases, the carrier frequency is about 2 × the square root of the disease prevalence. When one parent is a known carrier and the other has population risk, the child's risk is about the carrier frequency × 1/4; when both have population risk, it equals the disease prevalence, here 1/10,000. Hardy-Weinberg calculations assume a large population with random mating and no selection, mutation, migration or genetic drift.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 1 in 50 — 1/50 is the husband's chance of being a carrier; it omits the 1/4 chance that two carriers have an affected child.
  • B. 1 in 100 — 1/100 is the disease allele frequency (q), not a carrier probability or the child's risk.
  • D. 1 in 400 — 1/400 results from using the allele frequency (1/100) instead of the carrier frequency (1/50) for the husband.
  • E. 1 in 40,000 — 1/40,000 results from using the disease prevalence (1/10,000) as the husband's carrier risk.

Key point

When one parent is a known carrier of an autosomal recessive disease and the other has population risk, the child's risk equals the population carrier frequency (2pq, about 2q) multiplied by 1/4.

Memory hook

Recessive risk recipe: square-root the prevalence for q, double it for carriers, then quarter it for the baby.
