USMLE Step 1 practice question: Vitiligo: autoimmune CD8+ T-cell destruction of melanocytes driven by interferon-γ, contrasted with albinism and piebaldism

USMLE Step 1DermatologyImmunology

A 27-year-old woman comes to the physician because of white patches on her hands and around her mouth that have enlarged over 8 months. She has Hashimoto thyroiditis treated with levothyroxine. Examination shows sharply demarcated, depigmented, milk-white macules on the dorsal hands, fingertips, perioral skin and both knees. The macules show bright blue-white fluorescence under a Wood lamp. A few hairs within the patches are white. A skin biopsy specimen from a lesion shows complete absence of melanocytes with a sparse lymphocytic infiltrate at the margin.

Which of the following best explains these findings?

  1. CD8+ T-cell–mediated destruction of melanocytes
  2. Absent tyrosinase activity with normal melanocyte numbers
  3. Failure of melanocyte precursors to migrate from the neural crest
  4. Hypopigmentation from Malassezia inhibition of tyrosinase
  5. Postinflammatory loss of melanin transfer to keratinocytes
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. CD8+ T-cell–mediated destruction of melanocytes

Acquired, progressive, sharply demarcated milk-white macules on the hands, fingertips and periorificial skin that enhance under a Wood lamp, with white hairs and an associated autoimmune thyroid disease, indicate nonsegmental vitiligo. It is an autoimmune disease: melanocytes under oxidative stress release antigens and danger signals, and interferon-γ produced by autoreactive CD8+ cytotoxic T cells induces keratinocytes to secrete CXCL9 and CXCL10, which recruit more T cells that kill melanocytes. Biopsy therefore shows absent melanocytes, with lymphocytes at the advancing edge. Vitiligo is associated with autoimmune thyroiditis, type 1 diabetes, pernicious anemia and alopecia areata. Treatment includes topical corticosteroids or calcineurin inhibitors, narrowband UVB phototherapy and the topical JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib, which blocks interferon-γ signaling. Oculocutaneous albinism has normal melanocyte numbers but absent tyrosinase activity and is present from birth. Piebaldism is a congenital, stable failure of melanoblast migration from the neural crest due to KIT mutations, often with a white forelock. Tinea versicolor causes scaly hypopigmentation through azelaic acid made by Malassezia, and postinflammatory hypopigmentation is incomplete and follows prior inflammation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Absent tyrosinase activity with normal melanocyte numbers — Albinism is congenital, with melanocytes present that cannot make melanin; here melanocytes are absent.
  • C. Failure of melanocyte precursors to migrate from the neural crest — Failed neural crest migration (piebaldism) is congenital and stable, unlike this acquired, enlarging disease.
  • D. Hypopigmentation from Malassezia inhibition of tyrosinase — Tinea versicolor causes fine scaling and hypopigmentation, not milk-white depigmentation with absent melanocytes.
  • E. Postinflammatory loss of melanin transfer to keratinocytes — Postinflammatory hypopigmentation follows prior inflammation and leaves melanocytes in place.

Key point

Explain vitiligo as autoimmune CD8+ T-cell destruction of melanocytes, contrasted with albinism and piebaldism

Memory hook

Milk-white patches glowing under a Wood lamp in a woman with Hashimoto thyroiditis: cytotoxic T cells killed the melanocytes.
