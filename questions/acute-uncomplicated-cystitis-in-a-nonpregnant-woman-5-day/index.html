USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Acute uncomplicated cystitis in a nonpregnant woman: 5-day nitrofurantoin

USMLE Step 2 CKMicrobiologyInternal Medicine

A 27-year-old woman comes to the physician because of 2 days of dysuria, urinary frequency, and urgency. She has no fever, chills, flank pain, vaginal discharge, or vaginal itching. Her last menstrual period was 2 weeks ago, and a urine pregnancy test is negative. She has taken no antibiotics in the past 6 months and has no drug allergies. She had one similar episode 3 years ago. Temperature is 36.9°C (98.4°F), and other vital signs are normal. There is mild suprapubic tenderness and no costovertebral angle tenderness. Urine dipstick is positive for leukocyte esterase and nitrites. Local surveillance data show that 25% of Escherichia coli urinary isolates are resistant to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.

Which of the following is the most appropriate pharmacotherapy?

  1. Nitrofurantoin 100 mg twice daily for 5 days
  2. Ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 7 days
  3. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole twice daily for 3 days
  4. Amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for 7 days
  5. Urine culture, with antibiotics only after the result
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Nitrofurantoin 100 mg twice daily for 5 days

Dysuria, frequency, and urgency without fever, flank pain, or vaginal symptoms in a healthy nonpregnant woman indicate acute uncomplicated cystitis, a bladder-limited infection usually caused by Escherichia coli. In a typical presentation, symptoms plus a positive dipstick are enough for diagnosis; a urine culture is not required before treating a first or infrequent episode.

The IDSA first-line options are nitrofurantoin monohydrate/macrocrystals 100 mg twice daily for 5 days, a single 3-g dose of fosfomycin, or, if local resistance is below 20% and the drug has not been used in the previous 3 months, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole twice daily for 3 days. Nitrofurantoin reaches therapeutic concentrations only in urine, so it works for cystitis but should not be used for pyelonephritis or when the eGFR is below about 30 mL/min. Fluoroquinolones are effective but are reserved for more serious infections because of collateral resistance and adverse effects (tendinopathy, aortic aneurysm, QT prolongation, C. difficile infection). Beta-lactams such as amoxicillin are less effective, and aminopenicillin resistance among E. coli is high.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 7 days — Fluoroquinolones are reserved for more serious infections because of resistance and serious adverse effects, and 7 days is longer than needed.
  • C. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole twice daily for 3 days — Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is first-line only when local resistance is below 20%; here it is 25%.
  • D. Amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for 7 days — Amoxicillin is not recommended for empiric treatment because many E. coli isolates are resistant and cure rates are lower.
  • E. Urine culture, with antibiotics only after the result — In a typical uncomplicated case, culture is unnecessary before treatment, and delaying antibiotics only prolongs symptoms.

Key point

Acute uncomplicated cystitis in a nonpregnant woman is treated with 5 days of nitrofurantoin, single-dose fosfomycin, or 3 days of TMP-SMX if local resistance is below 20%.

Memory hook

Bladder-only bug: nitrofurantoin for five days or fosfomycin once. TMP-SMX only under 20% resistance; quinolones stay on the bench.
