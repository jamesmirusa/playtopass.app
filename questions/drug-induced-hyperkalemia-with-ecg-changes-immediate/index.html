USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Drug-induced hyperkalemia with ECG changes: immediate intravenous calcium

USMLE Step 2 CKRenalInternal Medicine

A 74-year-old man is brought to the emergency department because of progressive generalized weakness and lightheadedness over the past day. He has heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and stage 4 chronic kidney disease. Medications are lisinopril, spironolactone, carvedilol, and furosemide. Five days ago, he began taking trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for a urinary tract infection. Pulse is 46/min, respirations are 16/min, and blood pressure is 102/60 mm Hg. He is alert. Muscle strength is 4/5 in all extremities. An ECG shows sinus rhythm at 46/min with peaked T waves, low-amplitude P waves, a PR interval of 240 ms, and a QRS duration of 152 ms. A blood sample has been sent for a metabolic panel; results are pending.

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. Intravenous regular insulin with dextrose
  2. Intravenous sodium bicarbonate
  3. Oral sodium zirconium cyclosilicate
  4. Intravenous calcium gluconate
  5. Emergency hemodialysis
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Intravenous calcium gluconate

This patient almost certainly has severe hyperkalemia. He has advanced CKD and takes three drugs that raise potassium: an ACE inhibitor and spironolactone (reduced aldosterone production and action) plus trimethoprim, which blocks the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) in the collecting duct in an amiloride-like manner, decreasing the lumen-negative potential that drives potassium secretion. The ECG shows the typical progression of hyperkalemia: peaked T waves → PR prolongation and P-wave flattening → QRS widening, which can degenerate to a sine-wave pattern, ventricular fibrillation, or asystole. Bradycardia and muscle weakness (impaired neuromuscular excitability) complete the picture. Hyperkalemia makes the resting membrane potential less negative, which inactivates fast sodium channels and slows conduction.

When hyperkalemia causes ECG changes, IV calcium is the first treatment and should be given immediately — without waiting for the laboratory potassium value. Calcium raises (makes less negative) the threshold potential, restoring the gap between resting and threshold potentials and stabilizing the cardiac membrane within 1–3 minutes; the effect lasts 30–60 minutes, and calcium does not lower serum potassium. A typical dose is 10% calcium gluconate 10 mL (1 g) IV over 2–3 minutes (or calcium chloride through a central line), repeated if the ECG changes persist after 5 minutes. Next, potassium is shifted into cells (regular insulin 5–10 units IV with 25 g of dextrose if glucose is <250 mg/dL; nebulized albuterol 10–20 mg; sodium bicarbonate only with concomitant metabolic acidosis) and removed from the body (loop diuretics, potassium binders such as sodium zirconium cyclosilicate or patiromer, and hemodialysis for refractory hyperkalemia or kidney failure). Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, lisinopril, and spironolactone should be stopped.

High-yield summary: Hyperkalemia with ECG changes → (1) stabilize the membrane: IV calcium first; (2) shift: insulin + glucose, β₂-agonist (bicarbonate if acidotic); (3) remove: diuretics, binders, dialysis. Common drug causes: ACE inhibitors/ARBs, MRAs, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, heparin, beta blockers.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Intravenous regular insulin with dextrose — Insulin with dextrose lowers serum potassium within 15–30 minutes by stimulating Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, but it does not protect the myocardium immediately; in a patient with ECG changes, it is given after calcium.
  • B. Intravenous sodium bicarbonate — Sodium bicarbonate shifts potassium only modestly, mainly in patients with metabolic acidosis; it is not a first-line agent and does not stabilize the cardiac membrane.
  • C. Oral sodium zirconium cyclosilicate — Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate exchanges potassium in the gut and begins to work in about 1 hour; it is a removal strategy that is too slow for life-threatening conduction abnormalities.
  • E. Emergency hemodialysis — Hemodialysis is the most effective method of potassium removal for refractory hyperkalemia or kidney failure, but it takes time to arrange; membrane stabilization with calcium must come first.

Key point

In a patient at risk for hyperkalemia whose ECG shows conduction abnormalities (peaked T waves, PR prolongation, QRS widening), give IV calcium immediately to stabilize the myocardium before laboratory confirmation, then shift and remove potassium.

Memory hook

Peaked T waves? Calcium before chemistry: shield the heart first, then shift K⁺ into cells (insulin) and get it out (binders, dialysis).
