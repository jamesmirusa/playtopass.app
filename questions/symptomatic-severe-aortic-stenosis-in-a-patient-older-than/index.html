USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Symptomatic severe aortic stenosis in a patient older than 80 years: transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement

USMLE Step 2 CKCardiovascularInternal Medicine

An 82-year-old woman comes to the physician because of shortness of breath on exertion and two episodes of near-fainting while climbing stairs over the past 3 months. She has hypertension and chronic kidney disease (eGFR 52 mL/min/1.73 m²) and lives independently. Her pulse is 72/min, and blood pressure is 128/78 mm Hg. There is a grade 4/6 late-peaking crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur at the right upper sternal border radiating to both carotid arteries, a single second heart sound and a delayed carotid upstroke. Echocardiography shows an aortic valve area of 0.7 cm², a mean gradient of 52 mm Hg, a peak velocity of 4.6 m/s and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 60%. CT angiography shows iliofemoral arteries suitable for catheter access. The heart team estimates her surgical risk as intermediate.

Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

  1. Balloon aortic valvuloplasty as definitive treatment
  2. Repeat echocardiography in 6 months
  3. High-intensity statin therapy to slow valve progression
  4. Surgical replacement with a mechanical aortic valve
  5. Transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: E. Transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement

Exertional dyspnea and presyncope with a valve area below 1.0 cm², a mean gradient of at least 40 mm Hg and a peak velocity of at least 4 m/s define symptomatic severe aortic stenosis. Once symptoms appear, average survival without valve replacement is only about 2 to 3 years, so replacement is indicated regardless of ejection fraction. In the 2020 ACC/AHA valvular heart disease guideline, patients older than 80 years (or with a life expectancy under 10 years) and no anatomic contraindication should receive transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) in preference to surgery; surgical replacement is favored in younger patients, and the choice between them in ages 65 to 80 is shared with the heart team. Balloon valvuloplasty gives only transient relief and is a bridge, not definitive therapy. Surveillance echocardiography is for asymptomatic disease. Statins do not slow calcific aortic stenosis, as shown in several randomized trials. A mechanical prosthesis requires lifelong warfarin and is avoided at this age.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Balloon aortic valvuloplasty as definitive treatment — Valvuloplasty relieves obstruction only briefly and restenosis is the rule; it is a bridge or palliation.
  • B. Repeat echocardiography in 6 months — Watchful waiting is for asymptomatic severe stenosis; symptoms make replacement urgent.
  • C. High-intensity statin therapy to slow valve progression — Randomized trials showed that statins do not slow progression of calcific aortic stenosis.
  • D. Surgical replacement with a mechanical aortic valve — Surgery is preferred in younger patients, and a mechanical valve would require lifelong warfarin in an 82-year-old.

Key point

Choose transfemoral TAVR for symptomatic severe aortic stenosis in patients older than 80 years with suitable vascular access.

Memory hook

Symptoms plus severe AS: replace the valve; older than 80: through the femoral artery.
