USMLE Step 1 practice question: Kallmann syndrome: failed GnRH neuron migration from the olfactory placode

USMLE Step 1ReproductiveAnatomy

A 17-year-old boy is brought to the physician because of lack of pubertal development. He is concerned that his voice has not deepened and that, unlike his classmates, he has no facial hair. He was born at term after an uncomplicated pregnancy; at birth, he was noted to have a small penis, and ultrasonography showed an absent left kidney. His family reports that last year he did not notice the odor of a natural gas leak in their home. His height is at the 15th percentile, and his arm span exceeds his height by 6 cm. Vital signs are within normal limits. There is no facial, axillary, or chest hair. Genitalia are Tanner stage 1, and pubic hair is Tanner stage 2; each testis is 2 mL in volume. When he repeatedly opens and closes his right hand, his left hand involuntarily makes the same movements. Visual fields are full. Laboratory studies show: Luteinizing hormone 0.3 mIU/mL Follicle-stimulating hormone 0.5 mIU/mL Testosterone 25 ng/dL Prolactin 9 ng/mL TSH and free T4: normal Morning cortisol: normal Bone age is 13 years. Formal smell testing shows marked hyposmia. MRI of the brain shows absent olfactory bulbs and a normal pituitary gland.

Which of the following is the most likely cause of this patient's condition?

  1. Meiotic nondisjunction resulting in an extra X chromosome
  2. Deficiency of 5α-reductase type 2
  3. Loss-of-function mutation in the androgen receptor
  4. Prolactin-secreting adenoma of the anterior pituitary
  5. Failed migration of GnRH neurons from the olfactory placode
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: E. Failed migration of GnRH neurons from the olfactory placode

This boy has Kallmann syndrome: congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism with anosmia or hyposmia. Delayed puberty with low testosterone and inappropriately low LH and FSH indicates a hypothalamic or pituitary cause (hypogonadotropic hypogonadism). A normal prolactin, normal function of the other pituitary axes, and a normal pituitary on MRI point to isolated GnRH deficiency, and the impaired sense of smell with absent olfactory bulbs establishes Kallmann syndrome. Micropenis at birth reflects absent fetal LH-driven testosterone production in the second and third trimesters (after placental hCG declines). Eunuchoid proportions (arm span > height) result from delayed epiphyseal closure because of low sex steroids, whereas adrenarche (pubic hair) occurs normally because adrenal androgen production is not GnRH-dependent.

Embryology: GnRH-secreting neurons originate in the olfactory (nasal) placode, outside the central nervous system. During early fetal development, they migrate along olfactory, vomeronasal, and terminal nerve axons through the cribriform plate into the forebrain to reach the hypothalamus (preoptic area and arcuate nucleus). In Kallmann syndrome, mutations in genes that guide this migration and olfactory axon development (eg, ANOS1 [KAL1] on the X chromosome, which encodes the extracellular matrix protein anosmin-1; FGFR1; PROKR2; CHD7) disrupt both olfactory bulb formation and GnRH neuron migration. The resulting absence of hypothalamic GnRH secretion produces hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. X-linked (ANOS1) Kallmann syndrome is associated with bimanual synkinesis (mirror movements) and unilateral renal agenesis, both present in this patient.

Treatment is testosterone replacement to induce virilization; fertility can be achieved later with pulsatile GnRH or with hCG plus FSH to stimulate spermatogenesis.

High-yield summary: Delayed puberty + low LH, FSH, and testosterone + anosmia (± renal agenesis, mirror movements) = Kallmann syndrome, caused by failed migration of GnRH neurons from the olfactory placode.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Meiotic nondisjunction resulting in an extra X chromosome — Nondisjunction producing 47,XXY (Klinefelter syndrome) causes primary testicular failure (hypergonadotropic hypogonadism): small, firm testes with low testosterone but elevated LH and FSH, often with tall stature and gynecomastia. Smell is normal.
  • B. Deficiency of 5α-reductase type 2 — 5α-Reductase type 2 deficiency (46,XY) causes ambiguous or female-appearing external genitalia at birth because DHT is required for external genital development; virilization occurs at puberty as testosterone rises. LH and testosterone are normal to elevated, not low.
  • C. Loss-of-function mutation in the androgen receptor — Androgen receptor mutations (androgen insensitivity syndrome) cause a female or undervirilized external phenotype in a 46,XY individual, with elevated testosterone and LH due to loss of negative feedback. This patient has low testosterone and low gonadotropins.
  • D. Prolactin-secreting adenoma of the anterior pituitary — A prolactinoma can suppress GnRH secretion and cause hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, but prolactin would be markedly elevated, the pituitary would be abnormal on MRI, and smell would be normal.

Key point

GnRH neurons originate in the olfactory placode and migrate to the hypothalamus; failure of this migration (Kallmann syndrome) causes hypogonadotropic hypogonadism with anosmia or hyposmia.

Memory hook

Kallmann: GnRH neurons hitch a ride from the olfactory placode — miss the ride, lose both: no smell, no puberty (↓LH, ↓FSH).
