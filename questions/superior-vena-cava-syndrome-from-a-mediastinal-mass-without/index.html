USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Superior vena cava syndrome from a mediastinal mass without airway or cerebral compromise: tissue diagnosis before radiation or steroids

USMLE Step 2 CKHematology/OncologyInternal Medicine

A 61-year-old man who has smoked 2 packs of cigarettes daily for 40 years comes to the physician because of facial swelling and fullness in his head for 3 weeks, worse when he bends forward or lies down. His face and right arm are swollen, and dilated veins are visible over the upper chest. He has no stridor, hoarseness, confusion or headache. His pulse is 92/min, respirations are 18/min, and blood pressure is 134/82 mm Hg. Oxygen saturation is 96% on room air. CT of the chest shows a right paratracheal mass compressing the superior vena cava with bulky mediastinal lymphadenopathy.

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. Emergency mediastinal radiation therapy
  2. Biopsy of the mass or mediastinal lymph nodes
  3. High-dose intravenous dexamethasone
  4. Systemic thrombolytic therapy
  5. Anticoagulation alone with reassessment in 4 weeks
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Biopsy of the mass or mediastinal lymph nodes

Facial and arm swelling, positional fullness and dilated chest wall veins caused by a mediastinal mass indicate superior vena cava syndrome; in an older smoker, lung cancer (especially small cell) is the most likely cause, and lymphoma is the main alternative. Because collaterals develop over weeks, most cases are not emergencies. Without stridor, laryngeal edema or signs of cerebral edema, the priority is a histologic diagnosis, obtained by bronchoscopy with endobronchial ultrasound-guided needle aspiration or mediastinoscopy, because treatment depends on the tumor type: small cell lung cancer and lymphoma respond rapidly to chemotherapy, while non-small cell cancer may need radiation or stenting. Empiric radiation or corticosteroids before biopsy can obscure the histology, especially in lymphoma. Endovascular stenting gives the fastest relief and is used for severe or life-threatening symptoms. Thrombolysis is reserved for catheter-associated thrombosis, and anticoagulation alone does not address extrinsic tumor compression.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Emergency mediastinal radiation therapy — Radiation before biopsy can obscure the histology and is not needed without airway or cerebral compromise.
  • C. High-dose intravenous dexamethasone — Empiric corticosteroids can make lymphoma tissue nondiagnostic and do not treat a carcinoma.
  • D. Systemic thrombolytic therapy — Thrombolysis is for thrombosis, such as around a central catheter, not for extrinsic tumor compression.
  • E. Anticoagulation alone with reassessment in 4 weeks — Anticoagulation does not relieve compression by a tumor and delays diagnosis.

Key point

Obtain a tissue diagnosis first in superior vena cava syndrome without stridor or cerebral edema, reserving stenting for severe symptoms.

Memory hook

SVC syndrome without stridor: biopsy before burning or steroids.
