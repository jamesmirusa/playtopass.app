USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Empiric therapy for community-acquired bacterial meningitis in an adult older than 50 years

USMLE Step 2 CKNervousInternal Medicine

A 66-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department by her husband because of a 1-day history of fever, severe headache, and neck stiffness. This morning, she became increasingly drowsy and confused. She has hypertension and osteoarthritis. Medications are amlodipine and acetaminophen as needed. She has no known drug allergies. She has not been hospitalized recently and has had no neurosurgical procedures or head trauma. Temperature is 39.4°C (102.9°F), pulse is 112/min, respirations are 20/min, and blood pressure is 134/78 mm Hg. She is lethargic, opens her eyes to voice, and is oriented to person only. Passive flexion of the neck causes flexion of the hips and knees. There is no rash. Pupils are equal and reactive to light; funduscopic examination is limited. There are no focal motor deficits. Leukocyte count is 19,800/mm³. Two sets of blood cultures are obtained, and CT of the head is ordered before lumbar puncture.

Which of the following is the most appropriate empiric treatment at this time?

  1. Ceftriaxone and vancomycin
  2. Ceftriaxone, vancomycin, and dexamethasone
  3. Cefepime, vancomycin, and ampicillin
  4. Ceftriaxone, vancomycin, ampicillin, and dexamethasone
  5. Ampicillin, gentamicin, and acyclovir
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Ceftriaxone, vancomycin, ampicillin, and dexamethasone

This patient has acute bacterial meningitis: fever, severe headache, nuchal rigidity (positive Brudzinski sign), altered mental status, and leukocytosis. Because her level of consciousness is abnormal, IDSA guidance recommends head CT before lumbar puncture (other indications include immunocompromise, a history of CNS disease, new-onset seizure within 1 week, papilledema, and a focal neurologic deficit). When lumbar puncture is delayed for imaging, blood cultures are drawn and empiric antibiotics plus adjunctive dexamethasone are started immediately; therapy must never wait for the CT scan, because delayed antibiotic administration increases mortality.

Empiric therapy is selected by age and risk factors. In adults >50 years, the likely pathogens are Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Listeria monocytogenes, and aerobic gram-negative bacilli. The recommended regimen is vancomycin (to cover ceftriaxone-resistant pneumococci) plus a third-generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone or cefotaxime) plus ampicillin, because Listeria is intrinsically resistant to all cephalosporins. Dexamethasone (0.15 mg/kg IV every 6 hours for 2–4 days) is given 10–20 minutes before or together with the first antibiotic dose; it reduces mortality and neurologic sequelae (e.g., hearing loss) in pneumococcal meningitis and is continued only if S. pneumoniae is identified. If herpes simplex encephalitis is also suspected, acyclovir can be added, but it does not replace antibacterial coverage.

High-yield summary: suspected bacterial meningitis in a patient >50 years (or immunocompromised) → blood cultures, then immediately vancomycin + ceftriaxone + ampicillin + dexamethasone; obtain CT before LP only when indicated, and never delay therapy for imaging.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Ceftriaxone and vancomycin — Vancomycin plus ceftriaxone covers S. pneumoniae and N. meningitidis but not Listeria monocytogenes, which requires ampicillin in patients >50 years; adjunctive dexamethasone is also omitted.
  • B. Ceftriaxone, vancomycin, and dexamethasone — Adding dexamethasone is appropriate, but this regimen still lacks ampicillin; Listeria is intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins and is an important cause of meningitis after age 50.
  • C. Cefepime, vancomycin, and ampicillin — Vancomycin, ampicillin, and cefepime (or meropenem) are recommended for immunocompromised patients, and an antipseudomonal agent is used for health care–associated meningitis; she is neither, and this regimen omits dexamethasone, which is indicated for suspected pneumococcal meningitis in adults.
  • E. Ampicillin, gentamicin, and acyclovir — Ampicillin plus gentamicin is a neonatal regimen; it does not reliably cover penicillin- or ceftriaxone-resistant S. pneumoniae, the most common cause of bacterial meningitis in adults, and acyclovir treats only herpes simplex encephalitis.

Key point

Empiric therapy for suspected bacterial meningitis in adults >50 years is vancomycin, a third-generation cephalosporin, and ampicillin (for Listeria), with dexamethasone given before or with the first antibiotic dose; antibiotics should not be delayed for head CT.

Memory hook

Listeria targets the extremes — newborns, over-50s, immunocompromised — and shrugs off cephalosporins. So >50: vancomycin + ceftriaxone + ampicillin, dexamethasone before/with dose one.
