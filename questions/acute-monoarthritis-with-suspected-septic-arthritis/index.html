USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Acute monoarthritis with suspected septic arthritis: arthrocentesis before antibiotics

USMLE Step 2 CKMusculoskeletalEmergency Medicine

A 69-year-old man comes to the emergency department because of 2 days of pain and swelling of his right knee. Twelve days ago, he received an intra-articular glucocorticoid injection in the same knee for osteoarthritis. He has type 2 diabetes. Temperature is 38.6°C (101.5°F), pulse is 98/min, and blood pressure is 136/82 mm Hg. The right knee is warm, erythematous, and swollen with a large effusion; active and passive motion are severely limited by pain. Leukocyte count is 14,100/mm³, and C-reactive protein is 18 mg/dL (normal <0.8). Two sets of blood cultures have been drawn. Knee radiograph shows joint-space narrowing and osteophytes.

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. Empiric IV vancomycin and ceftriaxone, then MRI of the knee
  2. Oral prednisone for a presumed crystal arthritis flare
  3. Oral naproxen with review in 48 hours
  4. Intra-articular glucocorticoid injection for symptom relief
  5. Arthrocentesis for cell count, Gram stain, culture, and crystals
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: E. Arthrocentesis for cell count, Gram stain, culture, and crystals

An acutely hot, swollen joint with fever must be treated as septic arthritis until proven otherwise; recent intra-articular injection, diabetes, older age, and underlying joint disease all raise the risk, and Staphylococcus aureus is the most common organism. Untreated bacterial infection destroys articular cartilage within days and carries substantial mortality.

The diagnosis depends on synovial fluid. In a hemodynamically stable patient, arthrocentesis is performed before antibiotics, with cell count and differential, Gram stain, culture, and polarized light microscopy for crystals; the presence of crystals does not exclude coexisting infection. A synovial leukocyte count above 50,000/mm³ with neutrophil predominance strongly suggests infection, although lower counts do not rule it out. Empiric IV antibiotics are started immediately after joint aspiration and blood cultures, for example vancomycin, plus ceftriaxone if gram-negative organisms are possible, and then tailored to culture results. Treatment also requires drainage of the joint by repeated needle aspiration, arthroscopy, or arthrotomy. In a septic or unstable patient, antibiotics should not be delayed for aspiration.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Empiric IV vancomycin and ceftriaxone, then MRI of the knee — Antibiotics before aspiration can sterilize the synovial culture, and MRI is not needed to diagnose septic arthritis and would delay care.
  • B. Oral prednisone for a presumed crystal arthritis flare — Glucocorticoids could worsen an untreated joint infection, and crystal arthritis cannot be assumed without synovial fluid analysis.
  • C. Oral naproxen with review in 48 hours — Symptomatic treatment without a diagnosis risks irreversible cartilage destruction if the joint is infected.
  • D. Intra-articular glucocorticoid injection for symptom relief — Injecting a glucocorticoid into a possibly infected joint is contraindicated; the recent injection may itself be the source of infection.

Key point

In a stable patient with suspected septic arthritis, perform arthrocentesis for cell count, Gram stain, culture, and crystals before starting empiric antibiotics.

Memory hook

Hot joint: tap it before you treat it. Aspirate, culture, then antibiotics; crystals in the fluid do not clear the infection.
