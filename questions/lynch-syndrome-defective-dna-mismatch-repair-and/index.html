USMLE Step 1 practice question: Lynch syndrome: defective DNA mismatch repair and microsatellite instability

USMLE Step 1GeneticsPathology

A 42-year-old man comes to the physician because of 3 months of fatigue. Hemoglobin concentration is 8.9 g/dL with a mean corpuscular volume of 72 μm³, and stool testing for occult blood is positive. Colonoscopy shows a 4-cm ulcerated mass in the ascending colon and no other polyps. His mother was diagnosed with endometrial cancer at age 48 years, and his brother with colon cancer at age 45 years. Biopsy shows a poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma with many tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Immunohistochemistry shows absent nuclear staining for MSH2 in the tumor cells, and molecular testing shows variation in the length of short repetitive DNA sequences between tumor and normal tissue.

The protein that is absent in this patient's tumor normally participates in which of the following processes?

  1. Removal of ultraviolet light–induced pyrimidine dimers
  2. Joining of broken DNA ends without a template
  3. Repair of double-strand breaks using a sister chromatid
  4. Removal of deaminated bases by a DNA glycosylase
  5. Correction of base mismatches in newly synthesized DNA
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: E. Correction of base mismatches in newly synthesized DNA

Right-sided colon cancer before age 50 years with few polyps, relatives with early colon and endometrial cancer, loss of MSH2 staining and microsatellite instability indicate Lynch syndrome (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer). It is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by a germline mutation in a DNA mismatch repair gene: MLH1, MSH2, MSH6 or PMS2. After the second allele is lost in a cell, replication errors are no longer corrected. Mismatched bases and small insertion-deletion loops, which arise mainly where DNA polymerase slips on short tandem repeats (microsatellites), persist, so the length of these repeats varies and mutations accumulate in growth-regulating genes. The highest risks are for colorectal and endometrial cancer, followed by ovarian, gastric, small bowel and urothelial cancers. Colonoscopy every 1 to 2 years beginning at age 20 to 25 years is advised.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Removal of ultraviolet light–induced pyrimidine dimers — Nucleotide excision repair removes bulky lesions such as pyrimidine dimers; its failure causes xeroderma pigmentosum.
  • B. Joining of broken DNA ends without a template — Nonhomologous end joining repairs double-strand breaks; defects are seen in some forms of severe combined immunodeficiency.
  • C. Repair of double-strand breaks using a sister chromatid — Homologous recombination requires BRCA1 and BRCA2; defects predispose to breast and ovarian cancer.
  • D. Removal of deaminated bases by a DNA glycosylase — Base excision repair handles deaminated or oxidized bases; MUTYH defects cause a recessive polyposis syndrome.

Key point

Link Lynch syndrome to defective mismatch repair and microsatellite instability

Memory hook

Colon cancer at 42, mother with endometrial cancer, MSH2 missing: the DNA spell-checker is off and the microsatellites show it.
