USMLE Step 1 practice question: Simvastatin rhabdomyolysis after clarithromycin: CYP3A4 inhibition raises statin levels

USMLE Step 1PharmacologyPharmacology

A 66-year-old man comes to the emergency department because of diffuse muscle aches, weakness and dark urine for 2 days. He has hyperlipidemia treated with simvastatin 40 mg daily for 5 years without problems, and hypertension treated with lisinopril. Eight days ago he started a course of clarithromycin for community-acquired pneumonia. His pulse is 92/min and blood pressure is 132/80 mm Hg. Proximal muscles of the arms and legs are tender, and strength is 4/5. Serum creatine kinase activity is 18,400 U/L, and creatinine concentration is 1.9 mg/dL (baseline 1.0). Urinalysis shows a positive dipstick test for blood with no red blood cells on microscopy.

Which of the following best explains the development of this patient's condition?

  1. Displacement of simvastatin from plasma albumin
  2. Induction of hepatic glucuronidation of simvastatin
  3. Inhibition of CYP3A4-mediated simvastatin metabolism
  4. Inhibition of renal tubular secretion of simvastatin
  5. Additive inhibition of HMG-CoA reductase
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Inhibition of CYP3A4-mediated simvastatin metabolism

Myalgia, weakness, a creatine kinase level many times normal, acute kidney injury and a urine dipstick positive for heme without red cells (myoglobinuria) indicate statin-associated rhabdomyolysis. Simvastatin, lovastatin and, to a lesser degree, atorvastatin are metabolized by CYP3A4 and have extensive first-pass metabolism. Clarithromycin and erythromycin are strong CYP3A4 inhibitors, so starting one sharply raises statin exposure and the risk of myopathy; the simvastatin label contraindicates its use with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors. Other important inhibitors include azole antifungals, HIV protease inhibitors, verapamil, diltiazem, amiodarone and grapefruit juice. Safer choices are to hold the statin during the antibiotic course, to use azithromycin, which hardly inhibits CYP3A4, or to use a statin not dependent on CYP3A4, such as pravastatin or rosuvastatin. Treatment of rhabdomyolysis is stopping the statin and giving aggressive intravenous fluids. Protein-binding displacement rarely matters clinically, clarithromycin does not induce enzymes, and macrolides have no effect on HMG-CoA reductase.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Displacement of simvastatin from plasma albumin — Displacement from plasma proteins causes only transient changes in free drug and rarely causes toxicity.
  • B. Induction of hepatic glucuronidation of simvastatin — Enzyme induction would lower simvastatin levels; clarithromycin is an inhibitor, not an inducer.
  • D. Inhibition of renal tubular secretion of simvastatin — Simvastatin is cleared mainly by hepatic metabolism, not by renal tubular secretion.
  • E. Additive inhibition of HMG-CoA reductase — Macrolides do not inhibit HMG-CoA reductase; the interaction is pharmacokinetic.

Key point

Explain statin rhabdomyolysis caused by CYP3A4 inhibition from macrolides such as clarithromycin

Memory hook

Simvastatin plus clarithromycin: CYP3A4 jammed, statin piles up, muscles melt—use azithromycin or hold the statin.
