USMLE Step 1 practice question: High-altitude acclimatization: acetazolamide induces metabolic acidosis that increases ventilation

USMLE Step 1RespiratoryPhysiology

A 26-year-old man plans to climb Mount Kilimanjaro, ascending from 1800 m to the summit at 5895 m over 6 days. He had headache, nausea and poor sleep on a previous trip above 3500 m. His physician prescribes acetazolamide to start the day before ascent. At high altitude, the low partial pressure of inspired oxygen causes hypoxemia, which stimulates the peripheral chemoreceptors and increases ventilation; the resulting respiratory alkalosis then limits the increase in ventilation during the first days.

Which of the following best explains how acetazolamide helps prevent acute mountain sickness?

  1. It directly increases 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate in red blood cells
  2. It acts mainly by dilating the pulmonary arteries
  3. It stimulates erythropoietin secretion within hours
  4. It causes renal bicarbonate loss and a mild metabolic acidosis that increases ventilation
  5. It increases the affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. It causes renal bicarbonate loss and a mild metabolic acidosis that increases ventilation

At altitude, hypoxemia stimulates the carotid bodies and increases ventilation, but the fall in PaCO2 produces respiratory alkalosis, which inhibits the central and peripheral chemoreceptors and blunts further hyperventilation. Over several days the kidneys compensate by excreting bicarbonate, allowing ventilation to rise further; this is a key part of acclimatization. Acetazolamide inhibits carbonic anhydrase in the proximal tubule, causing bicarbonate diuresis and a mild metabolic acidosis that offsets the alkalosis and allows ventilation, and therefore PaO2, to rise sooner, especially during sleep. Other adaptations develop more slowly: increased 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate shifts the hemoglobin dissociation curve to the right within days, erythropoietin increases red cell mass over weeks, and hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction raises pulmonary artery pressure, which in excess causes high-altitude pulmonary edema. Gradual ascent is the main preventive measure; descent is the definitive treatment of severe altitude illness, and dexamethasone treats high-altitude cerebral edema.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It directly increases 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate in red blood cells — 2,3-BPG rises as part of acclimatization in response to alkalosis and hypoxia; acetazolamide does not directly increase it.
  • B. It acts mainly by dilating the pulmonary arteries — Pulmonary vasodilators such as nifedipine are used to prevent high-altitude pulmonary edema; acetazolamide's main benefit is not vasodilation.
  • C. It stimulates erythropoietin secretion within hours — Erythropoietin rises with hypoxia over days to weeks, independent of acetazolamide, and increases red cell mass slowly.
  • E. It increases the affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen — Increased hemoglobin affinity (left shift) results from alkalosis; acetazolamide's acidosis shifts the curve the other way.

Key point

Explain how acetazolamide aids acclimatization to altitude by inducing metabolic acidosis

Memory hook

Breathe faster, blow off CO2, alkalosis brakes the breathing: acetazolamide dumps bicarbonate and releases the brake.
