USMLE Step 1 practice question: Liquefactive necrosis in brain infarction: hydrolytic digestion and cystic cavity formation

USMLE Step 1General PrinciplesPathology

A 72-year-old man with atrial fibrillation dies of aspiration pneumonia 4 weeks after sudden onset of left-sided weakness and neglect. He had stopped taking his anticoagulant 2 months before the stroke. At autopsy, the right cerebral hemisphere in the territory of the middle cerebral artery contains a soft, partly cavitated area filled with turbid fluid. Microscopic examination of the edge of the lesion shows loss of tissue architecture, numerous macrophages with foamy cytoplasm, and proliferation of astrocytes at the periphery. No collagenous scar is present.

Which of the following types of necrosis is most characteristic of this lesion?

  1. Liquefactive
  2. Coagulative
  3. Caseous
  4. Fibrinoid
  5. Enzymatic fat
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Liquefactive

Ischemic infarcts in most solid organs, such as the heart, kidney and spleen, undergo coagulative necrosis: proteins denature, including the cell's own lysosomal enzymes, so cell outlines are preserved for days before the tissue is replaced by a collagen scar. The brain is the exception. Ischemic injury there produces liquefactive necrosis, because brain tissue is rich in lipids and hydrolytic enzymes and has little supporting connective tissue; lysosomal enzymes released by neurons, glia and arriving neutrophils digest the tissue completely. Microglia and macrophages then phagocytose the myelin-rich debris and become foamy, and by a few weeks a fluid-filled cystic cavity remains, walled off by reactive astrocytes (gliosis) rather than by fibroblasts. Liquefactive necrosis is also the pattern of abscesses, where neutrophil enzymes digest tissue into pus.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Coagulative — Coagulative necrosis preserves tissue architecture and is typical of infarcts in solid organs other than the brain.
  • C. Caseous — Caseous necrosis is the cheese-like center of granulomas in tuberculosis and some fungal infections.
  • D. Fibrinoid — Fibrinoid necrosis is immune-mediated damage to vessel walls, as in vasculitis and malignant hypertension.
  • E. Enzymatic fat — Enzymatic fat necrosis follows release of pancreatic lipases, producing chalky calcium soaps in peripancreatic fat.

Key point

Recognize liquefactive necrosis as the pattern of ischemic injury in the brain

Memory hook

Every other organ infarcts into a firm scar; the brain dissolves into a cyst lined by astrocytes.
