USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Obstructing ureteral stone with infection: urgent decompression of the collecting system

USMLE Step 2 CKRenalSurgery

A 46-year-old woman comes to the emergency department because of 2 days of severe left flank pain that radiates to the groin, with nausea and shaking chills since this morning. She has type 2 diabetes mellitus. Her temperature is 39.2°C (102.6°F), pulse is 118/min, respirations are 22/min, and blood pressure is 94/58 mm Hg. There is marked left costovertebral angle tenderness. Leukocyte count is 19,400/mm3 and serum creatinine is 1.6 mg/dL. Urinalysis shows many leukocytes, positive nitrite, and positive leukocyte esterase. Noncontrast CT of the abdomen and pelvis shows a 7-mm stone in the proximal left ureter with moderate left hydronephrosis and perinephric stranding. Blood and urine cultures are obtained, and intravenous fluids and intravenous ceftriaxone are begun.

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. Tamsulosin and observation for spontaneous passage of the stone
  2. Urgent ureteral stent placement or percutaneous nephrostomy
  3. Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy within 24 hours
  4. Ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy and stone extraction now
  5. Intravenous antibiotics alone, with repeat CT in 48 hours
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Urgent ureteral stent placement or percutaneous nephrostomy

This patient has an obstructing ureteral stone together with signs of infection and sepsis: fever, tachycardia, hypotension, leukocytosis, pyuria, and positive nitrite. Infected urine trapped behind an obstruction behaves like an undrained abscess. Antibiotics penetrate an obstructed kidney poorly, and the condition can progress rapidly to septic shock and loss of the kidney.

The priority is therefore urgent decompression of the collecting system, with either a retrograde ureteral stent or a percutaneous nephrostomy tube, in addition to fluids and broad-spectrum antibiotics. The two drainage methods are considered equally effective; the choice depends on local expertise and the patient's stability.

Definitive treatment of the stone (ureteroscopy or shock wave lithotripsy) is deferred until the infection has been treated, because manipulating or fragmenting a stone in an infected, obstructed system can worsen bacteremia. Medical expulsive therapy with an alpha-1 blocker is appropriate only for an uncomplicated distal ureteral stone of up to about 10 mm in a patient with controlled pain, no infection, and adequate kidney function.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Tamsulosin and observation for spontaneous passage of the stone — Expectant management with tamsulosin suits an uncomplicated ureteral stone; infection with obstruction is an absolute indication for drainage.
  • C. Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy within 24 hours — Shock wave lithotripsy is contraindicated in the presence of untreated urinary infection and does not relieve the obstruction immediately.
  • D. Ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy and stone extraction now — Definitive stone removal is postponed until sepsis has resolved, because instrumentation in an infected system can worsen bacteremia.
  • E. Intravenous antibiotics alone, with repeat CT in 48 hours — Antibiotics without drainage are inadequate, because the drug reaches the obstructed collecting system poorly and sepsis can progress.

Key point

An obstructing ureteral stone with signs of infection requires urgent decompression (ureteral stent or percutaneous nephrostomy) plus antibiotics; definitive stone treatment is delayed.

Memory hook

Pus under pressure must be drained. Infected and blocked: tube first, stone later.
