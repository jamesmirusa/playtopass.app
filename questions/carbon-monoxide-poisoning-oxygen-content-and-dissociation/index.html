USMLE Step 1 practice question: Carbon monoxide poisoning: oxygen content and dissociation curve

USMLE Step 1RespiratoryPhysiology

A 46-year-old man is brought to the emergency department in January together with his wife and two children; all have had headache, nausea and dizziness since the morning. During a power outage the family ran a gasoline generator overnight in their attached garage. He is confused but follows commands. Pulse is 112/min, respirations are 22/min and blood pressure is 128/80 mm Hg. Pulse oximetry shows an oxygen saturation of 99% on room air. The lungs are clear to auscultation. Co-oximetry shows a carboxyhemoglobin level of 27%.

Compared with normal, which of the following best describes this patient's arterial PaO2, arterial oxygen content and oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve?

  1. Normal PaO2, decreased O2 content, curve shifted left
  2. Normal PaO2, decreased O2 content, curve shifted right
  3. Decreased PaO2, decreased O2 content, curve shifted left
  4. Decreased PaO2, normal O2 content, curve shifted right
  5. Normal PaO2, normal O2 content, curve unchanged
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Normal PaO2, decreased O2 content, curve shifted left

Several household members with headache, nausea and confusion after running a generator in an enclosed space indicates carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning. The pulse oximeter reads 99% because standard two-wavelength devices cannot distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin; co-oximetry is required.

CO binds hemoglobin with roughly 200 to 250 times the affinity of oxygen and occupies heme sites, so oxygen-carrying capacity and arterial oxygen content fall, much as in anemia. PaO2 reflects oxygen dissolved in plasma, which depends on alveolar PO2 and gas exchange; with normal lungs and room air, PaO2 is normal, and saturation calculated from PaO2 on a blood gas is falsely reassuring. CO bound to one heme also increases the oxygen affinity of the remaining heme groups, shifting the dissociation curve to the left, so the little oxygen that is carried is released poorly in the tissues. CO additionally inhibits cytochrome c oxidase (complex IV), worsening cellular hypoxia and causing lactic acidosis.

Treatment is 100% oxygen by nonrebreather mask, which greatly shortens the half-life of carboxyhemoglobin; hyperbaric oxygen is considered for severe poisoning, such as loss of consciousness, neurologic deficits or pregnancy. Survivors may develop delayed neuropsychiatric sequelae, and imaging may show bilateral globus pallidus lesions. Methemoglobinemia similarly lowers oxygen content with a normal PaO2 and a left-shifted curve.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Normal PaO2, decreased O2 content, curve shifted right — Oxygen content is indeed decreased, but CO shifts the curve to the left; a right shift is caused by acidosis, fever, hypercapnia or increased 2,3-BPG.
  • C. Decreased PaO2, decreased O2 content, curve shifted left — PaO2 depends on inspired oxygen and gas exchange, both normal here, so PaO2 is not decreased.
  • D. Decreased PaO2, normal O2 content, curve shifted right — PaO2 is normal and oxygen content is decreased, the reverse of this pattern; the curve shifts left, not right.
  • E. Normal PaO2, normal O2 content, curve unchanged — Although PaO2 and standard pulse oximetry are normal, CO-bound hemoglobin cannot carry oxygen, so oxygen content falls and the curve shifts left.

Key point

Carbon monoxide poisoning lowers arterial oxygen content and shifts the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to the left while PaO2 and standard pulse oximetry remain normal.

Memory hook

CO steals the seats, not the air: PaO2 normal, content low, curve shoved left, and the pulse oximeter lies.
