USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Inferior STEMI with right ventricular infarction and hypotension

USMLE Step 2 CKCardiovascularInternal Medicine

A 61-year-old man is brought to the emergency department by ambulance 50 minutes after the onset of crushing substernal chest pain and diaphoresis. He has hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia. Medications are lisinopril, metformin, and atorvastatin. He has smoked 1 pack of cigarettes daily for 35 years. En route, paramedics gave him chewable aspirin 324 mg and one dose of sublingual nitroglycerin 0.4 mg. On arrival, he is diaphoretic and lightheaded. Temperature is 36.8°C (98.2°F), pulse is 72/min, respirations are 20/min, and blood pressure is 78/48 mm Hg; blood pressure before nitroglycerin was 128/76 mm Hg. Oxygen saturation is 97% on room air. Jugular venous pressure is 11 cm H₂O, and the jugular venous pulse rises with inspiration. The lungs are clear to auscultation bilaterally. Heart sounds are regular without murmurs. ECG shows 3-mm ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF with ST-segment depression in leads I and aVL. A right-sided ECG shows 1.5-mm ST-segment elevation in lead V4R. The cardiac catheterization laboratory has been activated, and the interventional team will be ready in 20 minutes.

In addition to preparing for primary percutaneous coronary intervention, which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. Intravenous furosemide
  2. Intravenous metoprolol
  3. Intravenous bolus of 0.9% sodium chloride
  4. Intravenous nitroglycerin infusion
  5. Intravenous morphine
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Intravenous bolus of 0.9% sodium chloride

This patient has an acute inferior ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) — ST elevation in leads II, III, and aVF with reciprocal ST depression in I and aVL — complicated by right ventricular (RV) infarction, confirmed by ST elevation in right-sided lead V4R. The inferior wall and the RV are usually supplied by the right coronary artery, so a proximal right coronary occlusion commonly injures both. The classic triad of RV infarction is hypotension, elevated jugular venous pressure (often with a Kussmaul sign), and clear lung fields. The abrupt fall in blood pressure after a single dose of sublingual nitroglycerin is a characteristic clue: the ischemic, poorly contractile RV is highly preload dependent, and venodilation reduces RV filling and RV output and therefore left ventricular preload and cardiac output.

Primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the definitive therapy and must not be delayed (goal first medical contact–to–device time ≤90 minutes). While the catheterization laboratory is being prepared, ACC/AHA guidance supports treating hypotension caused by RV infarction without pulmonary congestion with intravenous isotonic crystalloid (e.g., 250–500 mL boluses with frequent reassessment of the hemodynamic response and for signs of pulmonary edema). If hypotension persists despite adequate filling, an inotrope (e.g., dobutamine) or a vasopressor is added. Preload-reducing drugs (nitrates, diuretics, morphine) and negative inotropes (beta blockers) should be avoided, and atrioventricular synchrony should be maintained (pacing for high-grade AV block), because RV filling depends on atrial contraction.

High-yield summary: inferior STEMI + hypotension + elevated JVP + clear lungs = RV infarction → record right-sided leads (V4R), give IV fluid boluses, avoid nitrates, diuretics, morphine, and beta blockers, and proceed to emergency reperfusion with primary PCI.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Intravenous furosemide — Furosemide reduces preload; in RV infarction it would further decrease RV filling and worsen hypotension. Diuretics are indicated for pulmonary congestion, which is absent here (clear lungs).
  • B. Intravenous metoprolol — Beta blockers reduce mortality after myocardial infarction, but in the acute phase they are contraindicated in patients with hypotension, signs of low cardiac output, or risk of cardiogenic shock; IV metoprolol would worsen this patient's shock.
  • D. Intravenous nitroglycerin infusion — Nitroglycerin precipitated the hypotension; nitrates are contraindicated in RV infarction and when systolic blood pressure is <90 mm Hg.
  • E. Intravenous morphine — Morphine causes venodilation and can worsen preload-dependent hypotension; it also delays absorption of oral P2Y12 inhibitors and is reserved for pain refractory to other measures.

Key point

In inferior STEMI complicated by right ventricular infarction (hypotension, elevated JVP, clear lungs, ST elevation in V4R), treat hypotension with IV fluid boluses and avoid preload-reducing drugs while proceeding without delay to primary PCI.

Memory hook

RV infarct = preload-dependent pump: fill the tank, don't drain it. Fluids yes; nitrates, diuretics, morphine no. Clue: hypotension, high JVP, clear lungs.
