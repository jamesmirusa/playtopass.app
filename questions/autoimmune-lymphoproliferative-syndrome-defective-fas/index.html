USMLE Step 1 practice question: Autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome: defective Fas-mediated (extrinsic) apoptosis of lymphocytes

USMLE Step 1General PrinciplesPathology

A 6-year-old boy is brought to the physician because of enlarged lymph nodes in his neck and armpits that have persisted since age 2 years. He has had two episodes of anemia with jaundice and one of easy bruising, which improved with prednisone. He does not have recurrent infections, fever or weight loss. His father had similar lymph node enlargement as a child. Examination shows nontender cervical and axillary lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly 5 cm below the left costal margin. Hemoglobin concentration is 9.8 g/dL with spherocytes on the smear, platelet count is 62,000/mm³, and the direct antiglobulin test is positive. Flow cytometry of blood shows an increased proportion of αβ T cells that express neither CD4 nor CD8. Lymph node biopsy shows reactive hyperplasia without malignancy.

This patient's condition is most likely caused by a defect in which of the following?

  1. Intrinsic apoptosis controlled by BCL-2 family proteins
  2. Extrinsic apoptosis through Fas (CD95)
  3. NADPH oxidase–dependent respiratory burst
  4. CD40 ligand–mediated immunoglobulin class switching
  5. FOXP3-dependent regulatory T-cell development
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Extrinsic apoptosis through Fas (CD95)

Chronic nonmalignant lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly beginning in early childhood, autoimmune cytopenias, an expanded population of CD4−CD8− (double-negative) αβ T cells and a similarly affected parent indicate autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome (ALPS). Most cases are caused by heterozygous, dominant-negative mutations in FAS (CD95). Binding of Fas ligand on activated T cells to Fas trimerizes the receptor, recruits FADD and activates caspase-8, which triggers the executioner caspases of the extrinsic apoptotic pathway. This pathway deletes repeatedly stimulated and self-reactive lymphocytes in the periphery; when it fails, lymphocytes accumulate and autoreactive clones persist, causing lymphoproliferation, autoimmune hemolytic anemia and thrombocytopenia, and a higher lifetime risk of lymphoma. Serum biomarkers include elevated vitamin B12, IL-10 and soluble Fas ligand. BCL-2 overexpression from t(14;18) blocks the intrinsic pathway in follicular lymphoma; NADPH oxidase defects cause chronic granulomatous disease; CD40 ligand defects cause hyper-IgM syndrome; and FOXP3 defects cause IPEX.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Intrinsic apoptosis controlled by BCL-2 family proteins — Inhibition of intrinsic apoptosis by BCL-2 overexpression is characteristic of follicular lymphoma, not ALPS.
  • C. NADPH oxidase–dependent respiratory burst — NADPH oxidase deficiency causes chronic granulomatous disease with catalase-positive bacterial and fungal infections.
  • D. CD40 ligand–mediated immunoglobulin class switching — CD40 ligand deficiency causes X-linked hyper-IgM syndrome with recurrent and opportunistic infections.
  • E. FOXP3-dependent regulatory T-cell development — FOXP3 deficiency causes IPEX: enteropathy, endocrinopathy (type 1 diabetes) and dermatitis in infant boys.

Key point

Explain autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome as a defect in Fas-mediated extrinsic apoptosis

Memory hook

Big nodes, autoimmune cytopenias, double-negative T cells: the Fas death switch is broken.
