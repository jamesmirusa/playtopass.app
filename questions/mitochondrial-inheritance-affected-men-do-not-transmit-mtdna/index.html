USMLE Step 1 practice question: Mitochondrial inheritance: affected men do not transmit mtDNA disease

USMLE Step 1GeneticsGenetics

A 26-year-old man is evaluated for episodes of headache, vomiting and transient left-sided weakness over the past year. He has had exercise intolerance since childhood and is short for his family. His serum lactate concentration is increased. A muscle biopsy specimen stained with modified Gomori trichrome shows ragged red fibers. Genetic testing confirms a point mutation in a mitochondrial tRNA gene, present in about 70% of mitochondrial DNA copies in his muscle. His mother has diabetes mellitus and sensorineural hearing loss, and his maternal aunt has migraine and short stature. He and his wife are planning to have children and ask about the risk to their offspring.

Which of the following is the most accurate counseling for this couple?

  1. Each child has a 50% chance of inheriting the disorder
  2. Each child has a 25% chance of being affected
  3. All of his daughters will be carriers, and his sons will be unaffected
  4. Sons will be affected and daughters will be unaffected
  5. His children will not inherit the mutation from him
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: E. His children will not inherit the mutation from him

Recurrent stroke-like episodes, lactic acidosis, exercise intolerance, short stature and ragged red fibers in a young man are typical of MELAS (mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis and stroke-like episodes), most often caused by a point mutation in the mitochondrial tRNA-Leu gene. Mitochondrial DNA is inherited only from the mother: the oocyte contributes essentially all mitochondria to the embryo, and paternal mitochondria in the sperm are eliminated after fertilization. An affected father therefore does not transmit the disorder to his children.

An affected or carrier mother can transmit the mutation to all of her children, sons and daughters alike. Severity varies widely because of heteroplasmy: cells contain a mixture of normal and mutant mitochondrial DNA, the proportion differs between tissues and between siblings, and symptoms appear only when the mutant fraction in a tissue exceeds a threshold. This explains why his mother and aunt have milder, different manifestations such as diabetes, hearing loss and migraine.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Each child has a 50% chance of inheriting the disorder — A 50% risk per child describes autosomal dominant inheritance from a heterozygous parent; mitochondrial disorders do not pass from father to child.
  • B. Each child has a 25% chance of being affected — A 25% risk applies when both parents carry the same autosomal recessive allele.
  • C. All of his daughters will be carriers, and his sons will be unaffected — Obligate carrier daughters of an affected man describe X-linked recessive inheritance.
  • D. Sons will be affected and daughters will be unaffected — Affected sons and unaffected daughters from an affected father would fit Y-linked inheritance, which does not cause this syndrome.

Key point

Mitochondrial DNA disorders are transmitted only through the mother to all of her children, with variable severity due to heteroplasmy; affected men do not transmit them.

Memory hook

Mitochondria ride in the egg, never in the sperm: mother to all, father to none.
