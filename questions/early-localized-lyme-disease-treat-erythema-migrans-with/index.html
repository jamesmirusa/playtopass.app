USMLE Step 2 CK practice question: Early localized Lyme disease: treat erythema migrans with oral doxycycline without serologic testing

USMLE Step 2 CKMicrobiologyInternal Medicine

A 41-year-old man comes to the physician in July because of a rash on the back of his right thigh that he noticed 3 days ago. He returned 10 days ago from a camping trip in coastal Connecticut. He does not recall a tick bite. He has had mild fatigue and a headache but no fever, palpitations, joint pain or facial weakness. He takes no medications and has no drug allergies. His temperature is 37.2°C (99.0°F), pulse is 76/min and regular, and blood pressure is 124/78 mm Hg. Examination shows a 12-cm, round, erythematous, nontender patch with central clearing on the posterior right thigh. The remainder of the examination, including cranial nerves, shows no abnormalities. An ECG shows sinus rhythm with a PR interval of 160 ms.

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. Enzyme immunoassay for Borrelia burgdorferi antibodies with reflex immunoblot before any treatment
  2. Intravenous ceftriaxone for 14 days
  3. Topical corticosteroid and reassessment in 2 weeks
  4. Oral doxycycline for 10 days
  5. Skin biopsy with culture for Borrelia burgdorferi
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Oral doxycycline for 10 days

An expanding erythematous patch at least 5 cm in diameter, often with central clearing, appearing 3 to 30 days after exposure in an endemic area during tick season is erythema migrans, the hallmark of early localized Lyme disease. Fewer than half of patients recall the bite of the Ixodes scapularis nymph. The diagnosis is clinical: according to the 2020 IDSA/AAN/ACR guideline, serologic testing is not recommended for typical erythema migrans because antibodies are frequently absent this early, and a negative result would be falsely reassuring. Recommended treatment is oral doxycycline for 10 days, or amoxicillin or cefuroxime axetil for 14 days; doxycycline also covers possible coinfection with Anaplasma phagocytophilum. Intravenous ceftriaxone is reserved for hospitalized patients with Lyme carditis (for example, high-grade atrioventricular block) and for some neurologic disease. This patient has a normal PR interval and no neurologic findings.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Enzyme immunoassay for Borrelia burgdorferi antibodies with reflex immunoblot before any treatment — Serology is often negative in the first weeks and is not needed when the rash is typical; waiting for results delays treatment.
  • B. Intravenous ceftriaxone for 14 days — Ceftriaxone is used for Lyme carditis requiring hospitalization and for selected neurologic manifestations, none of which is present.
  • C. Topical corticosteroid and reassessment in 2 weeks — A topical corticosteroid does nothing for a spirochetal infection and allows dissemination to the heart, joints and nervous system.
  • E. Skin biopsy with culture for Borrelia burgdorferi — Culture and biopsy are insensitive, slow and unnecessary when the clinical picture is classic.

Key point

Treat typical erythema migrans empirically with oral doxycycline without serologic confirmation

Memory hook

Bull's-eye rash in tick country: don't test, treat. Doxycycline, 10 days.
