USMLE Step 1 practice question: Hereditary spherocytosis: defects of spectrin, ankyrin or band 3 with high MCHC and splenic hemolysis

USMLE Step 1Hematology/OncologyPathology

An 8-year-old boy is brought to the physician because his parents noticed yellowing of his eyes during a recent cold. He has had similar episodes before. His mother had her spleen removed as a teenager and had her gallbladder removed at age 30 years. His spleen tip is palpable 3 cm below the left costal margin. Laboratory studies show: Hemoglobin: 10.2 g/dL Mean corpuscular volume: 80 μm³ Mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration: 37.5 g/dL Reticulocyte count: 9% Indirect bilirubin: 3.1 mg/dL Direct antiglobulin test: negative A peripheral blood smear shows many small, round red blood cells that lack central pallor. Flow cytometry shows reduced eosin-5-maleimide binding to red blood cells.

Which of the following is the most likely underlying defect in this patient's red blood cells?

  1. Deficiency of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase
  2. Defect in proteins linking the membrane to the cytoskeleton, such as spectrin or ankyrin
  3. Substitution of valine for glutamic acid in the β-globin chain
  4. Loss of glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored complement regulators
  5. IgG autoantibodies bound to the red blood cell surface
Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Defect in proteins linking the membrane to the cytoskeleton, such as spectrin or ankyrin

Hemolytic anemia with splenomegaly, a family history of splenectomy and gallstones, a high MCHC, spherocytes on the smear, a negative direct antiglobulin test and reduced eosin-5-maleimide binding indicate hereditary spherocytosis, usually inherited as an autosomal dominant trait. Mutations in ankyrin, β-spectrin, α-spectrin, band 3 or protein 4.2 weaken the vertical links between the lipid bilayer and the spectrin cytoskeleton. Pieces of membrane are lost as microvesicles, so the cells lose surface area relative to volume and become spheres; dehydration raises the MCHC. The rigid spherocytes cannot pass through the splenic sinusoids and are destroyed by splenic macrophages (extravascular hemolysis), producing indirect hyperbilirubinemia and pigment gallstones. Infection with parvovirus B19 can cause an aplastic crisis. Treatment includes folic acid, transfusion when needed and, for moderate to severe disease, splenectomy after age 6 years (or partial splenectomy) preceded by vaccination against encapsulated organisms; after splenectomy, Howell-Jolly bodies appear. Autoimmune hemolytic anemia also produces spherocytes but has a positive direct antiglobulin test.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Deficiency of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase — G6PD deficiency causes episodic hemolysis after oxidant stress, with bite cells and Heinz bodies rather than chronic spherocytosis.
  • C. Substitution of valine for glutamic acid in the β-globin chain — The sickle mutation causes sickled cells and vaso-occlusive crises, not spherocytes with high MCHC.
  • D. Loss of glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored complement regulators — Loss of GPI-anchored CD55 and CD59 causes paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, with intravascular hemolysis and thrombosis.
  • E. IgG autoantibodies bound to the red blood cell surface — Warm autoantibodies also cause spherocytes, but the direct antiglobulin test would be positive.

Key point

Identify hereditary spherocytosis as a membrane-cytoskeleton defect with high MCHC and negative direct antiglobulin test

Memory hook

Jaundice with colds, big spleen, mother without spleen or gallbladder, MCHC high, Coombs negative: the membrane skeleton is loose.
