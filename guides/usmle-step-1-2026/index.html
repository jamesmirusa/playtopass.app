USMLE Step 1 in 2026: format, pass/fail and a 12-week plan

Updated: 2026-11-19

Step 1 tests whether you can apply the basic sciences to clinical situations. It has been reported only as pass or fail since January 2022, and in May 2026 the exam moved to shorter blocks. Here is what that means for your preparation.

The exam at a glance

ItemStep 1 in 2026
WherePrometric test centers, one testing day of about 8 hours
BlocksFrom May 14, 2026: 14 blocks of 30 minutes, up to 20 questions each (previously 7 blocks of 60 minutes)
QuestionsUp to 280 multiple-choice questions, one best answer each; some are unscored pilot items you cannot tell apart
ResultPass or Fail only (for exams taken on or after January 26, 2022)
Fee$695 USMLE program fee for a 2026 eligibility period, plus an international test delivery surcharge if you test outside the United States and Canada

Fees and dates change. Check the official USMLE and ECFMG pages listed at the end before you register.

What pass/fail changes — and what it does not

You no longer get a three-digit score, so residency programs will look harder at Step 2 CK. But the passing standard is still a real bar, and a fail stays on your record. Treat Step 1 as an exam you prepare for properly, not a formality. The goal is a comfortable margin above the line, not a perfect score.

What Step 1 actually asks

Almost every question is a short clinical vignette. You read a patient story, then answer a question about the mechanism behind it: why a drug causes an adverse effect, which enzyme is missing, what the biopsy will show, which nerve was injured. Pathology, physiology and pharmacology run through nearly everything; microbiology, immunology, biochemistry, genetics, behavioral science, biostatistics and ethics appear throughout. Questions are organized by organ system, which is why good preparation is also organized by system.

A 12-week plan that works

This plan assumes you have finished your basic science courses and can study 6–8 hours a day. Stretch it if you study part-time.

  1. Week 1 — baseline. Take a timed self-assessment or a 40–60 question diagnostic. Note your three weakest systems. Set up daily spaced-repetition review and keep it under an hour.
  2. Weeks 2–8 — system by system. Spend about a week on each pair of systems (for example cardiovascular + respiratory). Each day: review content for 2–3 hours, then do 40–80 timed questions in 30-minute blocks of 20, then read every explanation, including for the questions you got right.
  3. Weeks 9–10 — mixed blocks. Switch to random, timed blocks across all systems. This is where pacing for the new 30-minute format becomes automatic: about 90 seconds per question.
  4. Week 11 — full-length rehearsal. Sit at least one full practice day under exam conditions, with breaks timed the way you will use them on test day.
  5. Week 12 — consolidate. Review your error log and weakest topics. Lighter days at the end; sleep matters more than one more block.

Five habits that move the needle

  • Questions over passive reading. Retrieval practice beats re-reading. Do questions early, not "after I finish the content".
  • Read every explanation. The wrong answers teach the differential you will need next time.
  • Keep an error log. One line per miss: what you chose, why it was wrong, the rule you will remember.
  • Space your review. Revisit facts just before you would forget them instead of cramming them once.
  • Practice in the real rhythm. 20 questions in 30 minutes, repeated, until the clock stops being stressful.
